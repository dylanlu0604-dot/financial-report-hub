Industry View In-Line
AI-driven specification upgrades are reshaping the economics of copper-clad
laminate (CCL), the core material in PCBs, and driving demand for higher-

performance copper foil and glass cloth. We expect the global CCL TAM to rise
from US$19B in 2025 to US$47B by 2030, implying a 20% CAGR (vs market
expectations at US$35-40B), with AI/data-center applications accounting for 90%
of incremental growth and nearly two-thirds of demand by decade-end.
Content, not units, is the key growth driver. We estimate rising material intensity
accounts for 84% of AI-related CCL growth, as next-generation compute and

networking platforms move to higher-layer-count PCBs, lower-loss CCL and
smoother HVLP copper foil. Our bottom-up model implies the HVLP4 market
grows from <US$50M in 2025 to US$2.8B by 2030, a 123% CAGR. Extending the
same framework further downstream, we forecast the global PCB TAM will rise
from US$58bn to US$135bn over 2025–30, an 18% CAGR.
Broadening the AI trade upstream. We think high-end CCL, HVLP copper foil and
advanced glass cloth have entered an earnings upcycle but have yet to reach full

earnings realization and peak-cycle conditions. AI-driven specification upgrades are
reducing the number of  qualified suppliers and supporting mix, pricing and margins,
while long qualification cycles, lower yields and equipment bottlenecks should slow
the supply response.

Executive Summary
Why read this report?
We provide a comprehensive understanding of what copper foil, and copper-clad
laminates are and how they fit into the PCB supply chain. In addition, we have built
a proprietary bottom-up model to model out the copper foil and copper-clad
laminates market (please reach out to us for the model).
We also broke down the copper foil, copper-clad laminate, and printed circuit
board content per AI system across the different Nvidia, AMD, TPU, and Trainium

architectures.
AI has completely overhauled the PCB supply chain, going all the way up to upstream
materials like CCL (key raw material used in PCB production), copper foil (used in CCL
production), as well as electronic glass cloth (used in CCL production) and others. ABF
substrates are also seeing similar tailwinds, but we have talked about that here, here, and
here. In this report, we focus primarily on CCL and copper foil, but also touch on glass

cloth.
We forecast the CCL market to grow at a 20% CAGR over 2025-30, to US$47B from
US$19B (2.5x). Unit and content growth both contribute. AI data center cluster
deployments lift volumes, while faster signal transmission speeds require upgraded CCL
to contain insertion loss and crosstalk, on top of a mix shift toward higher layer-count and
more complex PCBs.
Against this demand, we see high-end CCL as under-supplied. Only a handful of suppliers

globally are qualified for M8+ grades, including EMC, Panasonic, TUC, Doosan, and
Shengyi. Because CCL capacity is grade-agnostic in architecture, nameplate capacity
overstates effective high-grade supply, and M7/M8+ supply-demand is therefore difficult
to quantify with precision. The absence of a precise gap does not change the direction. We
expect high-end tightness to translate into firmer pricing and margin expansion for the
qualified incumbents.

In copper foil, we forecast the high-end HVLP4 market to reach US$2.8B by 2030, a
123% CAGR over 2025-2030, though off a de minimis base. HVLP4 (hyper very low
profile) foil is the enabling input for low-loss, high-speed CCL, and therefore a gating item
in the AI PCB supply chain.
Effective capacity rests with five suppliers today: Mitsui Kinzoku, Co-Tech, Furukawa,
Fukuda and Copper Foil Luxembourg at roughly 1,000t/month, with Mitsui alone at 41%

of 2026 capacity. Adding to that base is harder than nameplate suggests. Converting
HVLP2 lines is not one-for-one; the longer production cycle cuts effective output by  more
than 40%. New lines require 6-12 months to ramp, and sit behind delivery queues for
surface-treatment equipment concentrated among a small number of Japanese suppliers.
We model demand rising to 39.2kt in 2027 from 12.3kt in 2026 (+218% y/y), and further
growing to 52.0kt in 2028 (+33% y/y). That would leave the market in a deficit of 31% by

Beyond discovery: Why high-end CCL stocks still have room
to run
We believe the market continues to underestimate the extent to which AI
infrastructure investment will expand end demand for copper-clad laminate. Our 2030
CCL total addressable market estimate of US$47B is meaningfully above market forecasts
of US$35-40B.
The key difference lies not only in our assumptions for AI server and data-center unit
growth, but also in the increasing material intensity of next-generation computing and

networking platforms. Higher layer counts, larger and more complex PCBs, faster data-
transmission requirements, and the growing use of premium low-loss and high-speed
materials should drive CCL content per system materially higher. This is all made possible
with more advanced copper foil  with tighter surface-profile, signal-integrity, and reliability
requirements.
Structural demand is supported by capex. The spending backdrop still has more than a

year of visible growth in it. Our  house view on cash cloud capex now stands at US$1.2T in
2026 (+104% y/y) and US$1.6T  in 2027 (+38% y/y) vs. consensus at US$1.39T for 2027.
This compares with buyside expectations of US$1.8T+ (including financial and operating
leases), suggesting there could still be meaningful room for further upward consensus
revisions.
CCL content is growing faster than AI-system units. The demand driver is a specification
ladder, not a single product cycle, and that distinction is what makes the cycle durable.

Layer counts on AI server motherboards are increasing with each generation to support
additional routing space for more signals as AI boards become more complex, while rising
signal and thermal demands also support the adoption of higher grade CCLs. Crucially,
each rung multiplies rather than adds, because moving up a grade changes the resin
chemistry, the copper-foil profile and the glass-cloth grade simultaneously. Content per
rack can therefore keep rising even if unit shipment growth moderates. The continuing

migration toward lower-loss grades is another reason the earnings opportunity may have
further to run.

The real scarcity is qualified, high-yield capacity. High-speed laminate is not a
commodity that can be replaced solely on price; materials must be validated for signal

loss, thermal reliability, dimensional stability and compatibility with specific PCB
fabrication processes. For example, an upgrade to M8 grade CCL is not a simple
incremental upgrade from M7. It requires simultaneous improvements in low-loss resin,
smoother HVLP copper foil, higher-spec low-Dk glass cloth, and much tighter
manufacturing tolerances. These changes narrow the process window, reduce throughput
and yields, and increase scrap, inspection, and qualification costs. At the same time, the

pool of suppliers able to produce M8 reliably at scale is much smaller, while AI-server
demand is accelerating, creating significantly tighter effective supply. That is why the M8
CCL ASP is more than double that of M7 grade CCL (Exhibit 9).
Once a material has been designed into a high-volume platform, changing it can require
renewed signal-integrity work, reliability testing and PCB-process validation. That creates
design-in persistence, and we believe a supplier’s qualification at M9 (for example) should

be viewed as more than a one-generation design win; it creates a cumulative advantage for
the next material transition. By the time a CCL supplier is qualified for M9, it has already
demonstrated that it can meet demanding electrical, thermal, dimensional-stability, and
manufacturability requirements at commercial scale. It has also accumulated valuable
process data, refined production yields, and developed working relationships with the end
customer, PCB fabricators, and upstream material suppliers. When the end customer

begins evaluating M10 or higher-grade materials, the incumbent can build from a proven
technical and manufacturing base rather than starting the qualification process from
scratch.
This advantage is reinforced by earlier access to customer roadmaps and opportunities for
joint development. A qualified M9 supplier is more likely to understand the customer’s
next-generation loss budget, stack-up constraints, reliability requirements, and production
timeline, allowing it to tailor its M10 product before broader qualification begins.

Customers also have an incentive to work with proven suppliers because changing
materials can require renewed signal-integrity modeling, PCB-process adjustments,

Where are we in the cycle?
In our cycle framework, we place high-end CCL and HVLP copper foil at past the trough,
but still ahead of full earnings realization and peak-cycle conditions. Demand is being

driven by a multi-year migration toward higher layer-count PCBs and lower-loss materials,
while qualified supply remains constrained by long customer qualification cycles, lower
yields and upstream bottlenecks. We therefore expect mix, pricing and margins to improve
through 2027-28; however, the opportunity remains selective, as commodity-grade CCL
can stay oversupplied even as qualified high-end materials tighten, making this a stock-
selection cycle rather than a broad sector call.

Conclusion
We view the current demand cycle as being driven by a sustained rise in material
specifications rather than by a single product refresh. This distinction is important, as it
suggests the opportunity could be more structural and durable than previous electronics
cycles, which were often more dependent on unit shipments and shorter replacement
cycles.
In our view, the specification upgrades are also multiplicative rather than merely additive.

Moving to a higher-performance grade CCL typically requires simultaneous changes across
the material stack, involving more advanced resin systems, lower-profile copper foil, and
higher-grade glass cloth. At the same time, increasingly complex AI server and networking
architectures require PCBs with more layers, greater routing density, tighter signal-
integrity tolerances, and, in some cases, larger board dimensions. As a result, the value of
CCL, copper foil, and related materials used per system, or per rack, can continue to

increase even if the pace of server or rack shipment growth begins to moderate.
The main drivers of this content growth include:
• Migration toward higher-grade CCL. Rising data-transfer speeds, higher operating
frequencies, and greater thermal loads are increasing the need for low-loss, high-
speed CCL materials. As AI accelerators and networking systems move to faster
interconnect standards, electrical performance becomes increasingly sensitive to

dielectric loss, signal attenuation, and material consistency. This should support a
continued mix shift toward higher-grade CCLs with more advanced resin
formulations and tighter manufacturing requirements.
• Increasing PCB layer counts. AI servers, accelerator boards, and high-speed
switches require substantially more routing capacity to support a growing number
of processors, memory modules, power-delivery components, and high-speed
input/output channels. Higher layer counts allow designers to accommodate this

complexity while maintaining signal and power integrity. This increases the amount
of CCL and copper foil required per board and can also raise manufacturing
complexity and average selling prices.
Taken together, the continued migration toward higher-layer-count PCBs, lower-loss and
higher-speed CCL, more advanced copper foil, and potentially larger board formats should
support sustained growth in material content per system. We believe this specification-

driven upgrade cycle creates further revenue and earnings upside for the broader CCL and
copper-foil supply chain, particularly for suppliers that are already qualified on leading AI
server, accelerator, and networking platforms. This underappreciated content opportunity
is central to our positive industry view.

Elite Materials Co. (2383.TW, OW, PT NT$8,400)

major GPU and ASIC customer platforms. It continues to invest for future growth, with
capacity expected to grow from 5,850k sheets/month at the end of 2025 to 11,250k
sheets/month by the end of 2028, or 92% growth. Most of these capacity additions will
be to support advanced grade CCL demand (M8+) over the next few years, driven by rising

$416.24, respectively.

What are the key risks? 
• A slowdown in AI infrastructure investment over the next several years. 
Demand for high-end copper foil and copper-clad laminate is increasingly tied to AI 
servers, networking equipment, and data-center expansion. A moderation in 
hyperscaler capital spending, slower growth in AI workloads, or longer server 
replacement cycles could weaken demand and delay expected capacity utilization.
• Company-specific execution or yield challenges. Lower-than-expected yields 
could result in higher unit costs and a slower margin recovery. This may create a 
gap between reported profitability and market expectations, particularly during 
the qualification and initial mass-production stages.
• Failure to qualify for next-generation materials platforms. Suppliers must 
typically undergo customer qualification processes before their products can be 
used in new server, switch, and accelerator platforms. An inability to meet

• Constraints in upstream raw-material supply. Shortages in materials such as

• Faster-than-expected adoption of co-packaged optics. A more rapid transition to

assumed any specific movement in copper prices over the next few years. Instead, 
we have held copper prices constant at current levels.

Advanced AI PCB manufacturing requires a longer cycle
Advanced AI server PCB takes 2.2x longer to produce than a normal PCB: Based on our 
assumptions, the end-to-end lead time for an advanced AI PCB is approximately 100 days, 
or 14.6 weeks, compared with 46 days, or 6.6 weeks, for a conventional multilayer PCB. 
This represents an incremental lead time of 56 days, or eight weeks, meaning an AI PCB 
takes 2.2x as long to produce and deliver to the ODM. 
• Transportation lead times are assumed to be the same for both products, so the 
full difference reflects the longer manufacturing cycle for the advanced AI PCB. 
The additional lead time consists of approximately 15 days for glass-cloth 
production, 11 days for CCL production, and 30 days for PCB fabrication.

The primary driver is the greater manufacturing complexity of the advanced AI PCB.
Higher-performance materials, including low-Dk+ glass cloth and HVLP+ copper foil,
require longer production cycles, although these materials are produced in parallel and
therefore do not add to lead time sequentially. The largest difference arises during PCB
fabrication. Our estimate assumes an Nvidia-grade compute board with 22 layers and five

single lamination cycle.
Each additional HDI build-up cycle may involve another round of lay-up, lamination,
cooling, laser drilling, copper plating or via filling, imaging, and inspection before the next
layer sequence can begin. Advanced AI boards also require tighter layer-to-layer
registration, finer circuitry, stricter impedance control, and more extensive quality
assurance. Together, these requirements extend the manufacturing cycle and increase the

risk of yield loss or rework.

Regional PCB Supply Chain Scorecard
Investors often look at technology from a regional perspective, and so we
collaborated with various analysts covering different stocks to put together the below
scorecard, which we think captures the value of each company and how they position
relative to each other. We believe these six factors best capture how investors should
value these companies, in no particular order: 1) pricing power, 2) AI mix, 3) capital

efficiency, 4) market concentration, 5) supply tightness, and 6) China substitution risk.
• Pricing power. The ability to raise or maintain prices and mix through the cycle
without sacrificing volume. This is distinct from passing through input-cost
inflation, which most suppliers can do. It is the clearest evidence that a company
captures the value it creates rather than ceding it to customers. A score of 5
indicates pricing gains even in a downturn; a 1 indicates that price is the primary

basis of competition.
• AI mix. We assess the share of revenue and profit attributable to AI and
accelerated computing, including exposure to both ASIC- and GPU-based
platforms. The scoring is straightforward: a score of 5 indicates that substantially
all revenue and profit are AI-related, while a score of 1 indicates exposure of less
than 10%.
• Capital efficiency. Returns on invested capital and cash conversion measured
against the capital intensity required to grow. There are several companies that can

hold pricing power, sit in a tight market, and enjoy a genuine qualification moat,
but may differ on capital efficiency. We measure ROIC against cost of capital across
a full cycle rather than at a peak year.
• Market concentration. The degree of supplier consolidation, measured through
top-one and top-three market share, the number of suppliers customers could
realistically qualify, and the stability of those positions. Concentration matters
because it underpins many of the other factors: fragmented markets tend to

compete on price, making durable pricing power difficult to sustain.
• Supply tightness. The current and expected balance between demand and
available capacity, assessed through lead-time trends, customer prepayments and
long-term agreements. Tight supply can temporarily support pricing and margins,
making an otherwise ordinary business appear structurally stronger than it is. We
therefore place greater weight on the capacity pipeline than on current lead times:

capacity scheduled to come online over the next two years is often a better
indicator of exit-cycle margins than today’s order book.
• China substitution risk. The risk that qualified domestic Chinese suppliers or
localization policies displace revenue. This is primarily a terminal value
consideration: whether the company’s addressable market in China remains intact
over a five-year horizon. We assess China revenue exposure and its trend, the
qualification status of domestic competitors, the size and direction of the technical

gap, explicit localization targets, and barriers to entry such as tooling, intellectual
property, materials purity and export controls. The score is inverted: a 5 denotes
severe risk and is entered into the composite as 6 minus the score, reducing the
overall result.

Copper Clad Laminate (CCL): The Backbone of Electronic
Inter-connectivity
How big is the market and who are the key players?
The CCL market has gone through a few cycles over the past decade. CCL is a broader,
more standardized materials market, so market value reflects more than PCB unit
demand. It also captures the pass-through of copper foil, glass fabric and yarn, epoxy resin,
and other input costs, as well as shifts in mix from conventional FR-4 toward higher-value

low-loss, high-speed, high-frequency, and specialty materials. As a result, headline sheet
capacity can appear ample even when supply remains tight in the highest-performance
grades, where customer qualification limits effective availability.
During 2016-17, the CCL market benefited from both a broad electronics upcycle and
tightening upstream supply. Demand was supported by rising electronics content in
automobiles, greater adoption of smart-home appliances, and wider LED penetration. At

the same time, electrolytic copper foil capacity was diverted toward lithium battery
applications, while electronic-grade glass yarn and fabric, epoxy resin, and other inputs
also became scarce. This allowed laminate suppliers to push through several rounds of
price increases, lifting the CCL TAM from US$10B in 2016 to US$12B in 2017. The increase
reflected higher input costs and price pass-through as much as stronger underlying
demand. But through 2018-19, input supply normalized, smartphone volumes plateaued,

and the geopolitics disrupted ordering patterns, and a lot of the pricing uplift faded, which
left the market largely flat at US$12B.
Covid-19 drove a sharp reset in CCL demand and pricing in 2020-21. Production was
disrupted early in the pandemic, but demand rebounded in 2H20 as WFH and more time
at home lifted sales of PCs and home electronics, while automotive and smartphone
demand also recovered. Growth broadened in 2021 across consumer electronics, PCs,

servers, home appliances, and new-energy vehicles. The resulting strain on laminate and
raw-material supply led to several rounds of price increases and a marked improvement in
producer margins. The CCL TAM rose from US$11B in 2020 to US$19B in 2021. This was a
broad cyclical surge in demand and pricing, rather than the start of a structurally AI-driven
upcycle.
The tight market in 2021 was followed by a two-year inventory and capacity
correction. CCL suppliers added capacity in response to the shortage. This is evidenced by

key CCL suppliers' capex rising from US$397M in 2020 to US$560-570M in 2021-2022,
before falling to US$346M in 2023 (Exhibit 28). But by the time it came online, consumer
electronics demand had normalized, channel inventories were being reduced, and raw
material prices were falling.  Shipments and selling prices weakened in 2022, with
conditions deteriorating further in 2023 as excess capacity and intense competition
pushed prices down faster than production costs, leaving many producers loss-making.

The CCL TAM fell from US$19B in 2021 to US$16B in 2022 and US$13B in 2023. The
pressure was concentrated in standard and lower-end grades, while high-frequency, high-
speed, and other higher-value products continued to gain share.

The CCL market began a two-speed recovery in 2024-25, led by AI and automotive
electronics. Industry inventories normalized, consumer-electronics demand improved
gradually, and downstream customers resumed restocking. Demand from automotive

to continue shifting their portfolios toward higher-end products. As a result, commodity
FR-4 recovered only modestly, while low-loss and high-speed materials used in AI servers,

TAM series provided, the market grew from US$15B in 2024 to US$19B in 2025.
We expect to see a broader adoption cycle for AI and high-speed CCL over 2026-2030.
AI servers and accelerators require more, larger, and higher-layer-count PCBs, while next-
generation switches, silicon-photonics modules, storage infrastructure, edge AI, and rising

performance, greater dimensional stability, and higher reliability, driving demand for
customer-qualified high-speed materials. Based on our estimates, we forecast the CCL
TAM to reach US$47B by 2030, implying a CAGR of 20% from 2025.
We would therefore characterize the current CCL cycle as selectively bullish rather than
uniformly bullish. The more relevant indicators are high-end product mix, customer

qualification, manufacturing yield and pricing power in low-loss and high-speed grades, not
conventional laminate capacity utilization alone. Commodity CCL can remain oversupplied
and price-sensitive even while qualified AI-related materials tighten. This should make the
current cycle more company-specific, and potentially more durable, than the broad
pandemic-era surge.

Periods Description
Period I (2016-2018): Copper-
foil diversion and broad
materials tightness
CCL demand was mainly driven by smartphones, notebooks, and consumer electronics, with servers and
networking making up a relatively small portion of the mix. Most products were standard FR-4 and mid-Tg. The
step-up in 2017 was largely cost-driven, as electrolytic copper foil capacity shifted toward lithium battery foil and

electronic-grade glass fabric tightened, allowing suppliers to raise prices.
Period II (2018-2019):
Saturation and price give-back
Global smartphone volumes saturated and began to decline, while the trade war disrupted order patterns. At the
same time, copper foil and glass fabric supply normalized, reversing much of the pricing gains seen in 2017.
Continued capacity additions in standard-grade CCL in China kept industry returns at commodity-like levels, leaving

the market broadly flat in value terms for three years.
Period III (2019-20): The quiet
inflection - 5G, high-speed
servers and early HPC
China’s 5G base-station rollout and the server platform upgrade cycle marked the first real shift toward product
differentiation in CCL. Low-loss and high-frequency grades required more advanced materials, including PPO and
hydrocarbon resin systems, low-Dk glass fabric, and low-profile copper foil. Although the segment remained small,

it allowed suppliers to earn a premium for technical capability rather than compete solely on price. With that said,
total market value still fell in 2020, driven by weakness in smartphones, autos, and industrial applications in the first
half, along with multi-year low copper prices.
Period IV (2020-2022): Demand
shock and cost-push spike
Demand from notebooks, tablets, TVs, gaming, and networking surged at the same time that copper foil, epoxy
resin, and glass fabric were in short supply. Suppliers implemented multiple rounds of price increases, lead times

lengthened, and customers began double-ordering. Market value rose 73% to US$19B in 2021, although much of
the increase reflected cost pass-through and inventory build.
Period V (2022-2024):
Destocking and price reset
From 2H22, consumer electronics entered a sharp destocking cycle, China’s 5G rollout slowed, and copper, resin,
and glass costs fell in tandem. As an upstream input to PCB production, CCL felt the correction earlier than the rest
of the supply chain. Market value declined for two straight years, reaching a trough of US$13B in 2023. Standard

grades returned to intense price competition, while high-speed products were more resilient, supported by server
demand and the early build-out of AI infrastructure.
Period VI (2024-2026):
Bifurcated recovery turns broad-
based
The recovery began in 2024, driven by AI, high-end servers, and networking, while PC and broader consumer
demand improved only modestly. By 2025–26, the gap between high-end and standard grades had narrowed. Key
inputs for advanced CCL, low-Dk glass fabric, HVLP copper foil, and PPE resin, also become constraints on overall

industry output. As upstream suppliers shift production capacity toward higher-end, standard grades (ie. E-Glass)
tightened as well, allowing price increases to spread across the industry. Market value returned to its 2021 peak in
2025, but with a fundamentally different product mix.
Period VII (2026-30): AI diffusion AI/data center-related CCL content per rack continues to rise as boards become larger, layer counts increase, and
cableless architectures shift more of the interconnect into the PCB. Each new platform generation raises the

Structural growth drivers
Higher-grade CCLs reduce dielectric loss and improve signal integrity at the high
frequencies used by AI accelerators, HBM, PCIe, and high-speed networking. Taking
Nvidia’s platform roadmap as an example, the earlier eight-GPU HGX systems used M6/
M7-grade CCL across both the Ampere and Hopper generations. With Blackwell, M8
became the primary grade used in NVL72 systems. Rubin is expected to bring another

modest step-up: most PCBs should remain on M8, while selected boards, such as the
midplane, move to M8.5. Looking further ahead, we expect Feynman to drive another
upgrade toward M9/M10 materials (Exhibit 32).
The progression reflects the rising electrical and thermal demands of AI servers. As data
rates increase and power density climbs, the PCB laminate plays a more critical role in
maintaining signal integrity, managing heat, and ensuring system reliability.

However, better material does not reduce the number of electrical connections that
must be routed. AI boards are becoming larger and more complex, with wider memory
buses, more accelerator-to-accelerator links, dense BGA packages, and thousands of high-
speed signals. Designers therefore need additional signal layers to provide enough routing
space while maintaining controlled impedance, limiting crosstalk, and giving critical signals
clean ground references.

AI systems also require increasingly demanding power-delivery networks because
accelerators consume very high currents at low voltages. More power and ground planes
help reduce resistance, inductance, and voltage fluctuations, while also improving
electromagnetic shielding and heat spreading. Higher-grade CCLs and higher layer
counts are therefore complementary: the CCL supports faster signal transmission, while
the additional layers handle greater routing density, power demand, and overall system

complexity.

Rubin Ultra reopens the PTFE box
Based on our supply chain checks, Nvidia had initially planned to adopt PTFE CCL for the
very high-layer-count midplane in its Kyber architecture. With Kyber now delayed, and
Rubin Ultra no longer expected to use that architecture, as previously discussed, the focus
has shifted back to the Oberon architecture. Nvidia is again evaluating PTFE for Rubin
Ultra on Oberon, with adoption most likely to begin on the switch-tray boards, though

actual adoption remains uncertain. At this stage, our checks have not indicated that either
the midplane or the smaller in-tray boards will move to PTFE CCL.
Potential solutions:
• Hybrid PTFE: PTFE CCL cores (potentially with no glass cloth) paired with M9
prepregs. Here we see Shengyi camp competing against an Elite Material + Taiflex
(8039.TW, not covered) camp.
• Pure PTFE: modified PTFE CCLs engineered to improve processability.
• Pure M9: M9 built on Q glass, i.e. what we would call the “real M9.”

• Pure M10: the least likely of the four.
Advantages of PTFE
• Best signal performance: the lowest Dk and Df of the mainstream options, below
even standard M9-class CCLs. This matters most where insertion-loss budgets are
tightest, i.e. the highest speeds and highest layer counts.
• Stable electricals: the low Dk/Df hold up across frequency and temperature, and
PTFE absorbs very little moisture, so performance is stable in use.
• Less dependence on Q glass: because PTFE’s intrinsic dielectric constant is already

very low, reaching the target Dk requires less (and in some hybrid constructions,
none) of the expensive, supply-constrained Q glass. That is both a cost lever and a
supply-security benefit.
Disadvantages of PTFE
• Mechanically soft: low modulus makes it difficult to handle and to hold to
tolerance.
• Difficult lamination: PTFE does not flow like a thermoset, so bonding needs either
very high temperatures or lower-melting fluoropolymer (such as PFA

(polyfluoroalkoxy))/thermoset bond layers; its high, non-linear CTE stresses
copper adhesion and complicates layer-to-layer registration, and pressing typically
requires vacuum or an inert atmosphere with longer cycles.
• Difficult drilling: the soft, gummy resin tends to smear and can tear, and any
ceramic filler is abrasive on tooling. Also, the  hole wall will not accept electroless
copper without an added activation step (sodium etch or plasma) on top of normal

desmear.
• Higher cost and qualification risk: materials and fabrication are both more
expensive, the qualified supply base is narrower, and yields at very high layer
counts carry risk.

CCL price drivers
CCL pricing rises with each performance grade because the underlying material
requirements become more demanding. The transition from M2 to M10 typically involves:
• lower-loss resin systems,
• smoother copper foil,
• higher-specification glass cloth, and
• tighter tolerances for dielectric thickness, copper roughness, and material
consistency.
These improvements are necessary to support higher data rates and maintain signal

integrity, but they also increase both raw-material costs and formulation complexity.
Production costs also increase as manufacturing specifications become more stringent.
Higher-grade CCLs require tighter control over coating, lamination, curing, and inspection,
while narrower process tolerances can reduce throughput and yield. Defects or variations
that may be acceptable in lower-grade products can disqualify a sheet intended for high-
speed applications, resulting in higher scrap rates and quality-assurance costs. Customer

qualification is similarly more rigorous, covering electrical performance, thermal reliability,
dimensional stability, and compatibility with advanced multilayer PCB processes.
The resulting ASP progression, from 1.0x for M2 to around 17x for M10, which reflects a
cost curve that becomes increasingly steep at the highest grades. Incremental
improvements in dielectric loss and conductor-related signal loss require
disproportionately greater investment in materials, process control, equipment, and

technical expertise.
High-grade CCLs also tend to have lower production volumes and fewer qualified
suppliers, limiting economies of scale and supporting stronger pricing. As a result,
premium CCL pricing reflects not only higher value material inputs and higher production
costs, but also the value of enabling next-generation AI, networking, and high-performance
computing systems.

What are the key risks?
AI demand may be structural but not linear. Hyperscaler investment can remain on a
long-term upward trend while quarterly hardware demand remains volatile. Accelerator

launch delays, power and cooling constraints, networking bottlenecks, changes in server
architecture or a temporary digestion of previously installed capacity could shift PCB and
CCL orders between periods. Because CCL sits relatively far upstream in the supply chain,
inventory adjustments by PCB makers and distributors can amplify changes in end
demand. Investors should monitor customer inventory days, lead times, order
cancellations and PCB production rather than relying only on headline cloud capex.

Qualification leadership is valuable, but not permanent. Qualification creates
incumbency, but not entitlement. A supplier qualified for a leading material grade today
enters the next development cycle with meaningful advantages: validated resin and
copper systems, accumulated production data, established yields, familiarity with the
customer’s PCB process and earlier access to next-generation design requirements. These
factors should improve the probability of winning next-generation CCL business and can

shorten the time required to move from development to commercial production.
Nevertheless, each platform transition can partially reopen the competitive process. The
customer may change its loss budget, glass composition, copper-foil requirement, stack-
up, thermal specification or sourcing strategy, while also introducing additional suppliers
to reduce concentration risk. A delayed qualification, reliability failure, inconsistent batch
performance or inability to ramp qualified output can result in lost supply share. Given the

size and concentration of leading AI platforms, missing one major qualification can affect
revenue growth, product mix and margins for several quarters, even if the supplier
remains competitive elsewhere.

help competitors close the gap at higher grades.
Upstream bottleneck and constraints. At advanced CCL grades, the relevant bottlenecks
are no longer generic fiberglass cloth, copper foil and resin. Suppliers require low-Dk, or

quartz-based glass cloth; ultra-low-profile HVLP copper foil; and specialized low-loss resin
systems.
• Glass cloth is particularly difficult to expand because advanced products require
proprietary glass compositions, precise fiber drawing and highly uniform weaving
and spreading. Toyota Industry's limited monthly output of air-jet weaving looms,
would limit the output of new electronic-grade glass-cloth capacity expansion.
• High-end copper foil faces a similar constraint. As signal frequencies increase,

smoother copper is needed to reduce conductor loss, but the foil must still
maintain sufficient adhesion to the resin system through repeated thermal and
manufacturing cycles. This makes HVLP foil materially harder to produce than
conventional foil.
Upstream tightness is therefore a two-sided factor for CCL suppliers. Scarcity can support
ASPs and reinforce the value of qualified capacity, but it can also prevent manufacturers
from converting strong orders into shipments.

CPO adoption presents a longer-term risk to the premium CCL thesis. Its main
advantage is that it reduces the electrical losses premium laminate grades are designed to
address. By placing optical engines within millimeters of the switch ASIC, rather than
sending high-speed signals across longer PCB traces, CPO could reduce the need for ultra-
low-Dk/Df materials such as M7 to M10. In effect, part of the signal-integrity challenge,
and the associated value, would shift from the system board to the package substrate and

interposer, potentially moving some margin capture from CCL suppliers to substrate and
advanced-packaging players.
The risk is unlikely to be immediate. CPO still faces challenges around cost, yield,
reliability, and thermal management, while pluggable optics remain the dominant
architecture. We therefore view CPO as a multi-year risk to the pace of premium-grade
CCL adoption, rather than a near-term threat to the supply tightness expected in 2026.

Copper Foil
Market development and competition
For two decades, PCB/CCL copper foil has been both cyclical, tracking electronics
production, and technology-driven, with premium grades pulling away from commodity
foil as customers demand thinner, smoother, more consistent material. What has changed
is where the performance frontier sits.
Phase 1 (2016–2018): EV batteries drew attention away
Between 2016 and 2018, electric vehicles (EVs) became the industry's main source of

incremental growth as PC and smartphone demand plateaued. Most new investment
therefore flowed into EV battery foil, which created a short-lived circuit foil shortage in
2016–17 and allowed suppliers to raise prices. Circuit foil itself advanced more quietly
during this period, driven by interconnect and data-transmission requirements rather than
headline volume. The qualities that would later define premium foil — surface
smoothness, low defect rates, and high yield — were already beginning to improve.

Phase 2 (2018–2019): The circuit foil shortage eased
In 2018–2019, the supply-demand imbalance in the circuit foil market normalized as
capacity increased and demand across PCs, smartphones, and servers stagnated. Copper
prices and suppliers' processing fees fell sharply as a result, leading to two years of
declining profitability for circuit foil suppliers.
Phase 3 (2020–2022): The COVID-19 demand surge
During COVID quarantines, work-from-home needs drove a sudden surge in demand for

notebooks, tablets, gaming devices, servers, and networking switches. This created a
favorable pricing environment for circuit foil suppliers, who benefited not only from higher
volumes but also from accelerated material upgrades across the PCB/CCL supply chain as
data-transmission requirements rose. Suppliers focused on circuit foil — notably Co-Tech
— were able to pass higher copper costs on to customers and implement price increases,
which drove margin expansion.

Phase 4 (2022–2024): Inventory de-stocking
Price competition for standard circuit foil (HTE / partial RTF) intensified during this period.
End demand was subdued as the market worked through the excess inventory built up
during the WFH era, and new Chinese capacity pushed the commodity segment further
into oversupply. At the same time, a new premium driver emerged: AI infrastructure —
mainly high-end servers and switches — which requires ultra-smooth, low-profile copper

to limit signal loss.
Phase 5 (2025–Future): AI, HVLP scarcity, and premium leadership
AI tightened the premium segment, while the commodity segment shifted from broad
oversupply to benefiting from surging demand for general-purpose servers used in agentic

Bottleneck emerges?
HVLP4 (hyper very low profile) copper foil is the enabling material for the fastest copper
interconnects in AI infrastructure. It is laminated into high-speed copper-clad laminate
(CCL) and then fabricated into the high-layer-count PCBs — compute boards,  switch
boards and midplanes — that carry the newest high-speed signals at acceptable insertion
loss. The direct buyers are therefore CCL producers. End demand is pulled almost entirely

by the leading AI accelerator and networking programs.

HVLP4 copper foil supply remains more concentrated than demand and, in our view, is the
binding constraint. Mitsui Kinzoku (5706.T, covered by Yu Shirakawa) is the clear
incumbent leader, accounting for 41% of global HVLP4 capacity in 2026, and still the
largest at 33% in 2028, on our estimates, even as the field broadens. The most important
second source is Co-Tech, which is the fastest follower: from a negligible <50 tons/month
in 2025 capacity, we model it scaling to at least 200 tons by the end of 2026, 700 tons by

EoY 2027 and 800 tons by EoY 2028. This will bring Co-Tech's supply share to 21% in
2028 from only 13% in 2026, making it the second largest supplier to Mitsui.
Beyond these two, Japan's Furukawa (5801.T, covered by Yu Shirakawa) and Fukuda
(private), as well as Luxembourg's CFL, add incremental qualified tons, while a cohort of
Chinese and Korean entrants — primarily Tongguan, Defu and Lotte Energy Materials
(020150.KS, covered by Ryan Kim) — sits in our "Others" bucket and is racing to expand

capacity. We would stress that "catching up" in HVLP4 means clearing customer
qualification at the CCL/PCB level, not merely installing a line; the incumbents' lead in
yield and consistency is why our supply build stays top-heavy toward Mitsui and Co-Tech
even in the out-years.
Our HVLP4 model is built bottom-up: we take our rack- and switch-level shipment
forecasts and multiply by an engineered estimate of HVLP4 foil consumed per rack,
derived from board area, layer count, HVLP4 layer coverage, and areal density (1oz

default), then gross up net foil to consumed foil  and process scrap. On this basis, our
HVLP4 monthly demand estimate rises from 1,028 tons in 2026 to 3,270 tons in 2027
(+218% y/y) and 4,335 tons in 2028 (+33% y/y). This equates to a market TAM of US

Against our modeled average monthly global capacity of 1,080/2,495/3,600 tons in 2026-

undersupply gap then widens sharply in 2027 to 31%  and remains a meaningful 20%
shortfall in 2028. In short, our base case shows HVLP4 undersupply emerging in 2026,
deepening in 2027, and sustaining through 2028.
New capacity is also difficult to scale. Two structural constraints sit at the center of our

undersupply call. First, equipment lead times: the surface treatment step — the process
that gives the foil its ultra-low, ultra-uniform profile — relies on specialized lines from a
very small set of Japanese equipment makers (e.g., Mifune, New Long and Nishi Industry).
With essentially the whole industry trying to expand at once, delivery times for these
treaters have stretched, and every expansion — incumbent or newcomer alike — is
effectively queuing for the same tools, pushing the date at which new nameplate capacity

produces sellable HVLP4 well to the right. Second, throughput: HVLP4's real output per
unit time is inherently lower than that of RTF or lower-tier HVLP. Hitting the very low
profile and tight roughness tolerance requires the treatment lines to run slower and
within a much narrower process window, and first-pass yields are lower — so a given line
yields materially fewer tons of qualified HVLP4 per unit time than it would of lower
grades. The steep processing-fee premium HVLP4 commands over RTF and lower HVLP

tiers in our model (Exhibit 47) is a direct reflection of that added difficulty. The two
effects compound: even if a maker has ordered tools, the combination of delivery queues
and low as-run throughput caps how fast usable tons actually reach customers.
Still, we would flag a few downside risks to our call:
• First, overcapacity: a fast, subsidized ramp  could add qualified tons faster than we
assume and close the gap.
• Second, yield: better-than-expected yield and throughput gains would effectively

lift real capacity off the same equipment base and erode the deficit.
• Third — and least likely, in our view — a PCB design shift that removes the need
for HVLP4 foil altogether; we struggle to construct a realistic path to this, since
high-speed copper interconnect still requires low-loss foil.
• Fourth, on the demand side, a shortage of other critical materials or components
(advanced glass fabric, ABF substrate, memory, optics, etc.) could delay when

customers actually pull HVLP4 orders in, softening the near-term deficit even if
end-demand is intact.
• Fifth, slower-than-expected or cancelled data-center build-outs — the ultimate
demand driver — would lower rack and switch shipments and, mechanically,
HVLP4 demand across the forecast.
Beyond these, our estimates are sensitive to the per-rack foil assumptions, to ASP/mix,
and to how much nameplate capacity is genuinely HVLP4-qualified versus lower grade —

small changes in any of these could impact the balance.

Company overview
Elite Material Co.  was incorporated on 24 March 1992 in Taoyuan's Guanyin Industrial
District, where it still has its head office, and began by supplying conventional FR-4 thin
laminates and prepreg; it listed on the Taiwan Stock Exchange on 27 November 1998. In
2002, EMC introduced its Green Materials product line using a license from Hitachi

Chemical of Japan. By 2013, it had become the largest halogen-free CCL supplier in the
world, according to Prismark (a position it still holds). Since the beginning, EMC also
worked on HDI board materials, which paid off when it entered Apple's supply chain in
2009. After many years of working with Apple on ultra-thin prepreg and any-layer/mSAP
process control, it gave EMC the capability to develop CCL required for high-speed server
materials, and in 2017, EMC entered high-speed CCL and eventually became number one

by 2023, when AI server specifications pushed the whole industry up the M-grade ladder.
Key products
Copper-clad laminates (CCL) and prepreg are EMC’s two core products and are sold to
the same customer base. CCL forms the cured core layers of a multilayer PCB, while
prepreg is a semi-cured, resin-impregnated glass cloth used to bond those layers together.
EMC also operates a small mass-lamination business in Hsinchu, though its contribution is
negligible.

Product mix by grade. In 2H26, we expect M7 and above to account for 67% of the
product mix, in line with our margin outlook. We believe the mix will continue to shift
toward higher-grade products as AI server PCBs require increasingly advanced CCL. Given
EMC’s position as the leading global supplier of high-speed CCL, we expect this mix
upgrade to support continued margin expansion.
Total capacity expands from 4,200K sheets/month in 2023 to 11,250K sheets/month

in 2028, roughly 2.7x in 5Y. Additions are heavily concentrated in 2025-28. 2025 capacity
increased by 34% in 2025, 5% in 2026, will increase by 54% in 2027 and another 19% in
2028. China continues to be EMC's biggest production site, with an expected 8,750K
sheets/month ending CY28, which would account for 78% of total capacity, with Taiwan
accounting for 15%, Malaysia 7%, and the US <1%.

Key competitors
The competitive landscape varies by product grade. In commodity FR-4, the largest
volume suppliers include China’s Kingboard Laminates and Shengyi Technology, as well as
Nan Ya Plastics, with the top five producers accounting for more than 60% of the global
CCL market. However, this is not EMC’s primary market. EMC primarily plays in the high-
speed and low-loss materials space, and in this market, its closest competitors are Taiwan

Union Technology, Panasonic, Doosan Electro-Materials, and Shengyi Technology.
Key customers
EMC sells directly to PCB fabricators. As AI servers have become an increasingly
important part of its high-speed CCL business, its customer base has come to include
many of the world’s leading PCB manufacturers, including Gold Circuit Electronics,
Unimicron, Victory Giant, Wus Printed Circuit, Compeq, Shennan Circuits, TTM
Technologies, and Tripod Technology, among others.

These relationships are typically close and engineering-intensive, as EMC must tailor its
materials to each fabricator’s manufacturing process and the performance requirements of
the end platform. In most cases, however, the laminate specification is set or approved by

What are the key risks?
• AI demand is structural but volatile: Hardware/CCL orders can swing with delays,

bottlenecks and inventory digestion.
• Qualification creates an edge, not permanence: Incumbents benefit, but each
platform transition can reopen competition.
• Competition is rising: Shengyi/Nan Ya/Kingboard could pressure share, pricing and
margins, even initially via second-source positions.
• Upstream materials are constrained: Advanced glass cloth and HVLP copper foil
shortages support pricing but can cap shipments.
• CPO is a longer-term risk: It could reduce demand for premium CCL grades, but is

unlikely to materially affect 2026 tightness.
Price target and valuation methodology
Our price target for EMC is NT$8,400: This implies 20x CY28 P/E. Our price target is
also our base case scenario value. It is derived from our residual income (RI) model (similar
to the rest of our tech hardware universe).
To calculate the fair value of the stock, we use the company's beginning equity plus the
present value of all expected residual income of earnings in excess of cost of capital. The

RI is positive when ROAE is above the cost of capital and negative when it is below the
cost of capital. Our key assumptions include a cost of equity of 9.3% (beta of 1.0, equity
risk premium of 8.7%, and risk-free rate of 1.0%), a medium-term growth rate of 12% and a
terminal growth rate of 3%.
The 20x CY28 P/E implied by our price target is slightly above the past 3Y average +1SD
of 19x, but much lower than the 32x recent P/E peak. We deem this valuation as fair,

because EMC is benefitting from an earnings growth cycle fueled by stronger AI server
CCL demand, EMC's own mix improvement and continued  pricing adjustments, supporting
margin expansion from 29.8% in 2025 to 41.4% by 2028 and an EPS CAGR of 117% over
2025-2028, on our estimates. Our 20x CY28 P/E for EMC is also higher than 18.5x for
TUC, reflecting EMC’s leading position in the AI CCL market in terms of both technology
capabilities and production capacity.  The stock is trading at just 12.8x CY28 P/E, below

EMC's past 3Y average of 14x and Shengyi's current valuation of 21x CY28 P/E. Thus, we
view the risk reward as attractive.

Company overview
Taiwan Union Technology Corporation (TUC) was established in 1974 under its former
name, Taiwan Union Glass Industrial Co., Ltd., initially specializing in the production of
optical glass. In 1997, the company transitioned its business focus to the manufacturing of
copper-clad laminates (CCL) and prepregs. In 2001, TUC further expanded its capabilities

by introducing mass lamination services for customers. In December 2003, TUC was
officially listed on the Taiwan OTC market.
To better serve growing customer demand in Greater China, TUC established its Changshu
plant in Jiangsu, China, in 2004. Today, TUC has one production site (also R&D Center) in
Taiwan, one in Thailand (Chonburi) and two in China (Changshu and Zhongshan); it also
operates a global service network covering Taiwan, China, Japan, South Korea, the United

States, and Germany. Its total production capacity exceeds 2.3M sheets of laminates per
month.
TUC's product portfolio offers a broad range of advanced features, including high-speed
and low-loss performance, environmentally friendly solutions, lead-free compatibility,
halogen-free materials, low coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE), superior chemical and
thermal resistance, and solutions for rigid-flex applications.
Key products
As a CCL manufacturer, TUC is specialized in the R&D, manufacturing and sales of CCLs

and prepregs. CCL forms the cured core layers of a multilayer PCB, while prepreg is a
semi-cured, resin-impregnated glass cloth used to bond those layers together. TUC also
offers lamination service to its customers, though its contribution is negligible at only 3%
in 2025.
Product mix by grade: In 2025, M7 and above contributed 36% of TUC's total sales, M4-
M6 32%, HDI and M4 below  23% and others 9%. We expect TUC's M7 and above mix to
increase to 40%/42%/46% in 2026/2027/2028, primarily driven by AI servers and high-

end networking switches that require increasingly advanced CCLs and prepregs (M7 at
least). On our estimates, this healthy shift toward more high-end applications, along with
its pricing adjustments, should support TUC's gross margin expansion from 22.7% in 2025
to 30.7%/35.0%/36.0% in 2026/2027/2028.
Capacity expansion plans: In 2018-2024, TUC's CCL capacity remained stable at 2M
sheets per month. In 3Q25, TUC added 0.3M sheets per month of capacity at its newly

opened Thailand plant (Thailand 1-1), which brought its total monthly capacity to 2.3M
sheets. TUC is currently ramping up its Thailand 1-2 facility, which will bring total monthly
capacity to 2.6M by the end of 2026. Total capacity is expected to increase from 2.6M at
year-end of 2026, to 3.8M/5.3M by the end of 2027/2028 (+46%/+39% y/y).

Key competitors
The competitive landscape varies by product grade. In commodity FR-4, the largest
volume suppliers include China’s Kingboard Laminates and Shengyi Technology, as well as
Nan Ya Plastics, with the top five producers accounting for more than 60% of the global
CCL market. However, this is not TUC’s primary market either.
TUC is primarily involved in the high-speed and low-loss materials space, and it has a
particularly strong presence in the high-end networking switch CCL market (400G, 800G

and above); in this market, its closest competitors are EMC, Doosan Electro-Materials, and
Shengyi Technology. TUC was also a qualified supplier to AWS for the CCLs used in
Trainium servers. It was qualified in 1H25 and started supplying to the AWS supply Chain
from Trainium2 onwards. In the ASIC server market, TUC primarily competes with EMC
and potentially Panasonic as well.
It's also worth noting that TUC is the primary, if not sole, supplier of thick copper CCLs

used in power supply applications to a dominant data center power supplier solution
provider; this niche product falls in the 'Others' category, with 'Others' revenue mix rising
sharply from 8% in 2Q25 to 14% in 2Q26.
Key customers
TUC also sells directly to PCB fabricators. With high-end switches and servers being an
crucial part of its high-speed CCL business, TUC's customer base includes many of the
world’s leading server/switch PCB manufacturers, including Gold Circuit Electronics, ISU

Petasys (007660.KS, covered by Ryan Kim), Wus Printed Circuit (002463.SZ, not
covered), Shennan Circuits, TTM Technologies (TTMI.O, not covered), Tripod Technology
(3044.TW, not covered), First High Tech (5439.TWO, not covered), Shengyi Electronics
(688183.SS, not covered) etc.

Price target and valuation methodology
Our price target for TUC is NT$2,700: This implies 29x CY27 or 17x CY28 P/E. Our price
target is also our base case scenario value. It is derived from our residual income (RI)
model (similar to the rest of our tech hardware universe).
To calculate the fair value of the stock, we use the company's beginning equity plus the
present value of all expected residual income of earnings in excess of cost of capital. The

RI is positive when ROAE is above the cost of capital and negative when it is below the
cost of capital. Our key assumptions include a cost of equity of 9.3% (beta of 1.0, equity
risk premium of 8.7%, and risk-free rate of 1.0%), a medium-term growth rate of 11% and a
terminal growth rate of 3%.
The 17x CY28 P/E implied by our price target is in line with the past 3Y average +1SD of
17x. We deem this valuation as fair, because TUC is benefitting from an earnings growth

cycle fueled by stronger AI server/networking CCL demand, TUC's own mix improvement
and continued  pricing adjustments, which supports margin expansion from 22.7% in 2025
to 36.0% by 2028 and a EPS CAGR of 138% over 2025-2028, on our estimates. The stock
is trading at just 8.6x CY28 P/E, below TUC's past 3Y average -1SD of 9.5x. Thus, we view
the risk reward as attractive.

Company overview
Co-Tech Development Corporation (Co-Tech) was established in May 1998  to manufacture
electrodeposited (ED) copper foil for the copper-clad laminate (CCL) and printed circuit
board (PCB) industries. Plant 1 entered mass production in 2000 and Plant 2 followed in
2002,. Co-Tech registered on Taiwan's emerging stock board in 2007 and was formally

listed on the Taiwan OTC market in 2010; it changed its name to Co-Tech Development
Corporation in 2014.
Headquarters and the R&D centre are in Neihu Technology Park, Taipei, and all
manufacturing sits at a single site in Yunlin County, Taiwan, where Plant 1 and Plant 2
operate and Plant 3 is under construction. The current wholly-Taiwan manufacturing
footprint is a point of difference versus most peers, which carry China or Southeast Asia
capacity; it removes China tariff/localization exposure but concentrates operational risk in

one site.
Key products
Copper foil is the conductive layer of a CCL: the laminator bonds foil onto resin-
impregnated glass cloth to form the core, so foil surface roughness (Rz) sets the floor on
insertion loss at high frequency. Co-Tech's ladder runs from LP310/HG310 (standard to
mid-loss, outer layer) through the RT/RG reverse-treated family (RG311 at Rz ≤2.3µm,
RG312 at ≤2.1µm, and the more advanced RG313) into the HVLP series (VG410 at ≤1.5µm,

VL411 at ≤1.0µm, plus the more advanced PF51N (HVLP4+) and PF511 (HVLP5)).
Product mix by grade: Co-Tech fully stopped supplying conventional HTE and standard
RTF in 3Q26, redirecting all capacity to the RG (advanced RTF) and HVLP series. Co-Tech
estimates its RG series holds over dominant share of the general server market, while
HVLP is more aimed at AI servers and high-end switches. Today, we estimate the RG family
remains Co-Tech single largest revenue line at 73%, while HVLP revenue mix is at 14% in

2026. We expect Co-Tech HVLP3+ mix to reach 14%/34%/46% of sales in
2026/2027/2028, primarily driven by AI server boards and 800G/1.6T switches. This mix
shift plus processing-fee hikes should lift Co-Tech's gross margin from 22% in 2025 to
30%/40%/47% in 2026/2027/2028, on our estimates.

What are the key risks?
• AI demand is structural but volatile: Hardware/CCL orders can swing with delays,

bottlenecks and inventory digestion.
• Qualification creates an edge, not permanence: Incumbents benefit, but each
platform transition can reopen competition.
• Competition is rising: Chinese entrants could pressure share, pricing and margins,
even initially via second-source positions.
• Other materials are constrained: Advanced glass cloth shortages could also cap
copper foil and CCL shipments.
• CPO is a longer-term risk: It could reduce demand for premium copper foil grades,

but is unlikely to materially affect 2026 tightness.

Company overview
Kingboard Laminates (KB Laminates) is the laminates arm of the Kingboard Group, spun
out of Kingboard Holdings (0148.HK) and listed on the Main Board of the Hong Kong
Stock Exchange in December 2006. The operating history predates the listing by nearly
two decades. The group built its first laminate plant in Shenzhen in 1988 and began

producing paper laminates in 1989, then expanded horizontally into glass epoxy and
flame-retardant paper laminates and vertically into copper foil, glass yarn, glass fabric,
bleached kraft paper and epoxy resin, with the Fogang copper foil operation itself spun off
and listed in Singapore in 1999 as Kingboard Copper Foil. That upstream ownership
(vertical integration) is the defining feature of the business and the clearest contrast with
most other CCL suppliers globally. Kingboard self-supplies most of its critical inputs and

competes on cost, scale and supply security, whereas its competitors buy its inputs and
compete on specification.
KB Laminates positions itself as the world's largest CCL producer by volume, a position it
dates to the mid-2000s.  Kingboard Laminates remains controlled through the parent,
reports across the Laminates, Properties and Investments segments, and is headquartered
in Sha Tin, Hong Kong.
Key products
KB Laminates mainly reports across three business segments:

• Laminates
• Properties
• Investments
In 2025, laminates accounted for 99% of revenue and 98% of operating profits.
• Within the laminates business, we believe the majority of revenue comes from CCL
(80-90%), while the rest comes from upstream materials such as copper foil, glass
cloth, etc.
Capacity
The laminate segment's capacity footprint is concentrated in China. In Guangdong, the
core sites are Fogang in Qingyuan City (both glass epoxy and paper laminate plants, the

historical heart of the business), Shixing County in Shaoguan, Jiangmen in the Jianghai high-
tech zone, and Guangzhou. In Jiangsu, there is Kunshan in the economic and technological
development zone, plus Jiangyin for composite material.
Copper foil capacity mainly sits at Fogang and Lianzhou in Guangdong, with a new
21,000-tonne plant for high-frequency and low-loss foil starting mid-2027 in Fogang.

Price target and valuation methodology
Our price target for KB Laminates is HK$75: This implies 14x CY28 P/E. Our price  target
is also our base case scenario value. It is derived from our residual income (RI)  model
(similar to the rest of our tech hardware universe).
To calculate the fair value of the  stock, we use the company's beginning equity plus the
present value of all expected  residual income of earnings in excess of cost of capital. The

RI is positive when ROAE is  above the cost of capital and negative when it is below the
cost of capital. Our key assumptions include a cost of equity of 9.7% (beta of 1.0, equity
risk premium of 8.7%, and risk-free rate of 1.0%), a medium-term growth rate of 8% and a
terminal growth rate of 3%.
The 14x CY28 P/E implied by our price target is slightly above the past 3Y average  of
12.8x. We deem this valuation as fair, because KB Laminates is benefitting from an

earnings growth cycle fueled by price hikes of its FR4 CCLs and glass cloth, as well as its
gradual penetration into the higher-end CCL material market, which supports margin
expansion from 19.6% in 2025 to 41.7% by 2028 and a EPS CAGR of 91% over 2025-2028,
on our estimates. That said, we believe the company remains more of a cyclical play
compared to its Taiwanese peers including EMC and TUC. Thus, we view the 1x re-rating
vs. its past 3Y P/E average as fair. The stock is trading at just 8.5x CY28 P/E, below KB's

past 3Y average of 13x. Therefore, we view the risk reward as attractive.
Bull case value HK$115: In this scenario, we assume demand for general server,
networking switches, and AI servers sees significantly stronger growth in 2026-28, driven
by replacement and new data center builds. In addition, we also assume KB Laminates is
able to secure and take share in the high-speed, low-loss CCL market (M6+ grade CCLs),
while also able to continue to hike price well into 2027 with no signs of pricing declines

into 2028. Under this scenario, our bull case value implies 21.3x CY28 P/E.
Bear case value HK$33: In this scenario, we assume demand for general server,
networking switches, and AI servers declines vs our current expectations of growth, in
2027-28. And therefore KB Laminates is not able to take share in the high-speed, low-loss
CCL market, as leading incumbents compete on price to keep utilization rates full. We also
assume KB Laminates will have to start lowering prices for standard grade CCLs. Under

this scenario, our bear case value implies 6.1x CY28 P/E.

Copper-Clad Laminates (CCL) Primer

Introduction: What are copper-clad laminates (CCL)?
CCL is the major determinant foundation of all PCBs (printed circuit boards). It 
dictates signal integrity, reliability, manufacturability, and cost across all PCBs, 
ranging from consumer electronics to automotive to AI infrastructure hardware 
(server, storage, networking, etc). CCL is a composite. 
In the hierarchy of electronics supply chain, CCL serves as the fundamental building block 
for printed circuit boards (PCBs). It is a composite material created by reinforcing a resin 
matrix with a filler (typically fiberglass cloth) and cladding it with electrolytic copper foil 
on one or both sides. CCL is a critical proxy for downstream demand in high-growth 
sectors such as AI servers, 5G infrastructure and automotive electronics. 
Core composition and manufacturing
The performance of a CCL is dictated by its three primary components:
• Copper foil: This is the conductive layer that is etched to create electrical 
pathways for signals and currents. The industry is shifting toward ultra-low-profile 
copper foils for high-speed or high-frequency CCLs to minimize signal loss (ie. 
HVLP3+). 
• Reinforcement materials: In most cases, electronic-grade fiberglass cloth serves 
as the reinforcement material in a CCL, which provides mechanical strength and 
dimensional stability.
• Resin system: This is the "glue" of the laminate and mainly where the CCL 
suppliers differentiate from each other. In some high-speed applications, standard 
FR-4 (epoxy) is replaced by specialized resins like polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) 
or polyphenylene ether (PPE) to achieve lower dielectric constant (Dk) and 
dissipation factor (Df).

CCL powerhouses: Who’s leading the market?
There are three key specifications that determine a CCL supplier's tier in the supply chain:
1. Dielectric constant (Dk) measures the ability of the material to store electrical
energy. Lower Dk allows for faster signal transmission.
2. Dissipation factor (Df) represents the energy "lost" as heat. For AI and high-
frequency applications, ultra-low loss materials, which have very low Df, are the

common standard and command higher ASPs.
3. Glass transition temperature (Tg) is the temperature at which the material shifts
from a rigid to a rubbery state. High-Tg materials are essential for reliability in
heat-intensive environments like within servers.
The global CCL market is led by KB Laminates and Shengyi Technology in China; Elite
Materials (EMC), Nan Ya, ITEQ, TUC, and Chang Chun in Taiwan; Panasonic in Japan;
Doosan in Korea; and Isola in the US. KB Laminates and Shengyi together account for

roughly one-third of global CCL capacity, but both remain heavily weighted toward
conventional grade CCL. We estimate that less than 5% of KB Laminates’ capacity is
allocated to M6+ products, compared with approximately 5–10% for Shengyi.
In high-speed CCL, EMC, TUC, and Doosan have gained share from Panasonic in recent
years. The shift began in servers and high-end networking around 2020 and accelerated
from 2023 as these suppliers secured early qualifications for AI-related reference designs.

We estimate that EMC, TUC, Doosan, and Panasonic each allocate more than 50% of their
capacity to M6+ grades.

Why higher-grade CCL is disproportionately harder to 
produce
Advancing CCL performance is not a matter of upgrading a single input — it requires 
simultaneously re-engineering copper foil, resin, and glass fiber, each of which becomes 
progressively harder to produce and integrate as electrical requirements tighten.
Resin –  two competing requirements in one material system. As copper foil surfaces are 
smoothed to reduce signal loss, resin formulation must solve two problems at once rather 
than one. Lower Dk/Df increasingly requires low-polarity resin chemistries, but the same 
low polarity that improves electrical performance also reduces the resin's natural ability 
to bond to copper and glass. With the mechanical grip previously provided by rough foil

### Page 94

M

94
no longer available, adhesion must be re-engineered from scratch — through coupling
agents, functional end-groups, and in some cases metallic interlayers. Because these two
requirements pull in opposite directions within the same formulation, qualifying a new
resin/foil combination at HVLP3/HVLP4 level is an inherently slow, iterative process.
Glass fiber –  trading a well-understood material for a harder one. The same dynamic
applies to glass fiber reinforcement. Lowering Dk/Df means moving away from E-glass —

a chemically well-behaved, decades-optimized material — toward compositions that are
either harder to melt and draw into fiber consistently (NE-/NER-glass) or harder to handle
without breakage (quartz/Q-glass). In both cases, the sizing treatment that bonds glass to
resin must also be redeveloped rather than carried over from lower grades.
Lamination –  tighter process windows, less margin for error. These upstream changes
converge at the CCL manufacturer's lamination step. Bonding a low-adhesion foil to resin

using the same temperature, pressure, and cure-time profile used for standard foil risks
voids or delamination. Producers must instead adjust lamination profiles and use resin
systems specifically formulated for compatibility with low-profile foil — reinforcing why
advancing an M-grade tier requires changing resin, foil, and glass together, rather than
substituting a single component.
Net effect, structurally lower yields and a narrower supplier base. The cumulative result

of these compounding constraints is lower production yields, longer and more iterative
qualification cycles, and a small pool of suppliers capable of meeting spec reliably at scale.
This explains both the substantial cost premium commanded by higher-grade CCL and the
persistent lag between capacity additions and demand: the constraint is not simply
building more production lines, but building lines capable of consistently meeting a
materially tighter specification.

The high bar for CCL qualification

Qualification for high-grade CCL, typically M7 and above, can take 12 to 18 months with
major customers such as Nvidia, AWS, Google, and Meta. The process is demanding
because it evaluates more than whether the laminate meets its datasheet specifications. A
supplier’s internal quality control typically measures properties such as Dk/Df, peel
strength, and Tg on flat coupons from a controlled sample batch. Customer qualification,
by contrast, tests the material after it has been drilled, plated, laminated, and built into

the customer’s actual board design using the designated PCB fabricator’s process.
Performance must also remain consistent across multiple production lots, rather than a
single optimized sample.
This distinction matters because signal integrity and reliability in a finished board can
differ materially from what flat-laminate testing suggests. Via-stub reflections, glass-
weave skew on the actual trace geometry, and voiding caused by narrow lamination

windows may only emerge during board-level testing. Customers also apply tighter,
application-specific acceptance criteria than standard industry specifications, reflecting the
high cost of a field failure in an AI server.
CCL capacity is fungible, not equivalent
CCL capacity is fungible mainly because the manufacturing process is essentially
grade-agnostic. Whatever the grade, you are doing the same three things:
1. impregnating woven glass cloth with resin on a treater to make prepreg,

2. stacking that prepreg with copper foil, and curing the stack under heat and
pressure in a lamination press,
3. then trimming and inspecting.
A standard FR-4 line and a low-loss line are the same machines in the same sequence.
What differs is the recipe, not the architecture.
That means switching grades is closer to a bill-of-materials swap than a capital project.
You change the resin system (epoxy for FR-4, PPE/PPO or hydrocarbon blends for low-

loss, higher-Tg or halogen-free chemistries for others), the glass cloth (E-glass versus low-
Dk NE- or Q-glass), and the copper foil (standard versus reverse-treat or HVLP/ULP for
high-frequency loss budgets). You also retune press profiles, cure temperature, and
vacuum. All of that lives in the process recipe and the purchasing order book, which is why
CCL suppliers can and do reallocate lines toward whatever grade is earning the best

profit.
The fungibility is asymmetric, though, and this matters. A line qualified for high-end
material can trivially drop down and run commodity FR-4, despite it being over-qualified
for the job. However, the reverse is not as straightforward. Upgrading a commodity line
often requires better vacuum lamination, tighter thickness and resin-content control,
cleaner handling to avoid particulate defects, and frequently a dedicated treater to
prevent resin cross-contamination. So capacity flows downhill freely, and requires capex

and time going uphill.
The most important consequence for output is that fungible does not mean
equivalent. High-end grades consume more line time per unit of area: longer and hotter
press cycles, thinner cores that mean more panels for the same copper area, more
rigorous AOI and electrical testing, and materially lower first-pass yield during ramp. The
same physical asset therefore produces meaningfully fewer square meters per month when
it is running ultra-low-loss material than when it is running consumer FR-4, the penalty is

often on the order of tens of percent rather than a rounding error.
Layered on top is a commercial friction that is slower than the engineering one. CCL is
qualified into an OEM's approved-vendor list at the level of a specific grade produced on a
specific line at a specific site, with reliability testing behind it. Requalifying a line typically
takes quarters, not weeks, and PCB fabricators themselves have to requalify their own
process on the new laminate. So the theoretical swing capacity in an industry model is real

but arrives with a lag, and it arrives unevenly across customers.
Fungibility at the CCL step also does not fix a bottleneck sitting upstream of it. The
binding constraints in a high-end cycle are usually low-Dk glass cloth, HVLP copper foil,
and specialty resin, inputs with concentrated supply, long qualification cycles, and 12- to
24-month capacity lead times. A producer can reallocate every press it owns toward high-
end product and still be capped by how much low-Dk cloth it can source. Adding CCL

capacity into that situation raises utilization risk without raising effective supply.
Putting it altogether: Because capacity is fungible, a demand pull in one grade does not
sit contained within that grade, it propagates. Reallocation toward high-end material
shrinks industry area output measured in square meters while expanding revenue and
gross-profit capacity, since price per square meter for advanced grades runs multiples of
commodity. It simultaneously crowds out lower grades, which is why tightness that

originates in AI server boards shows up as lead-time extension and price increases in
unrelated end markets like automotive and consumer PCB. From a modeling perspective,
area-based nameplate capacity can overstate effective supply during a product-mix shift.
Press-hours, or area-equivalent capacity adjusted for grade-specific cycle times and yields,
provide a better measure, although these inputs are company-specific and generally
confidential.

The major differences
Structural versus pull-forward demand: AI infrastructure is a multi-year investment
cycle; pandemic demand was driven by temporary work-from-home and consumer-
electronics purchases.
This cycle is narrow and content-led, not broad and anonymous. 2021 was every
consumer device shipping at once, reaching CCL through two layers of distributor and PCB
inventory. This comes from a concentrated handful of hyperscalers and accelerator

roadmaps that specify the grade directly: far better visibility, far worse concentration.
Capacity only counts if it is qualified. In 2021 any line relieved the shortage. Now it has
to be a qualified position at grade (M8, M8.5, M9) with a 12-18 month qualification cycle,
so the 2021 response of adding standard capacity does nothing for the tight part of the
market.
The constraint sits further upstream and clears more slowly. Copper, resin and E-glass

were tight in 2021 and cleared inside roughly 18 months. Low-Dk and HVLP foil are near-
monopoly inputs with new capacity in 2H27 at the earliest, behind CCL equipment on two-
year lead times.
Margins are dispersing rather than rising together. The pandemic lifted everyone's
utilization. Profitability now turns on qualified mix and glass fabric allocation, which
makes this a stock selection cycle rather than a sector one.
The similarities
Demand is again outrunning available supply in the most constrained product

categories.
Strong demand visibility encourages capacity expansion by both CCL producers and
upstream material suppliers.
Capacity takes time to respond; tightness is cascading into grades unrelated to the
driver. Standard FR-4 inflated on generic input shortages then and is being squeezed by AI
allocation now. Non-driver end markets pay for it, and that slice of the price gain reversed
first last time.
Higher-end suppliers benefit disproportionately through better product mix, utilization

and margins.
If it breaks, it breaks the same way. The cycle could ultimately overshoot, with
aggressive investment and inventory building creating future oversupply and price
pressure. A platform slip or de-spec would unwind through the 2H22 route: standard
grades destock first, prices follow inputs down, and CCL feels it before the rest of the
chain because it sits upstream of the PCB.

Copper Foil Primer
What is electro-deposited copper foil?
Electro-deposited (ED), or electrolytic,  copper foils are mainly used in lithium batteries
(battery foil) and PCB/CCLs (circuit foil). The production process of electrolytic copper foil
primarily involves dissolving copper into an electrolyte, depositing it as a very thin
continuous sheet on a rotating drum, peeling it from the drum, surface-treat it and slit   it
to the customer's required width. The basic process platforms for circuit foil and battery

foil are similar, but the downstream requirements are pretty different.
• Battery foil is coated with anode material and transports electrons during charging
and discharging.
• Circuit foil is laminated to resin and glass fabric to make CCL, and then etched into
conductive tracks for signals and currents.
Therefore, a circuit foil producer optimizes thickness, surface profile, adhesion and  signal
transmission. For high-speed/high-frequency applications, the focus shifts from simple

conductivity to signal integrity and adhesion physics. As signal frequencies increase, the
"skin effect" (Exhibit 101) becomes the dominant challenge. At high frequencies, electrons
no longer 'flow' through the center of copper but instead crowd toward the surface, which
means the signal has to travel a longer, jagged physical distance if the surface is rough
(resembling a mountain range under microscope), leading to significant signal loss.

The PCB/CCL BoM typically specifies one of the following grades based on the required
profile, which is usually surface roughness, measured as Rz.
• HTE (High Temperature Elongation): The standard product. Good for 1-2L PCBs but
too rough for high-speed PCBs/CCLs.
• RTF (Reversed Treated Foil): The rough side is flipped to face the bonding layer,

while the signal-carrying side is smoother. This is a cost-effective middle ground,
but the surface roughness typically cannot meet the requirements of very high-
speed PCBs/CCLs.
• VLP (Very Low Profile): This is significantly smoother than the previous two but
also more expensive.
• HVLP (Hyper Very Low Profile): This is the standard for AI server and high-end
networking PCBs/CCLs, enabling sharp reduction of signal attenuation.
Key Technical Trade-offs: Rough-surfaced copper anchors into the resin system like a hook.

When you move to HVLP copper foil, the physical bond weakens, making adhesion
difficult. CCL suppliers must use chemical treatments to ensure the copper does not de-
laminate during soldering; the mastery of such treatment is a key testament to a CCL
supplier's manufacturing capability.
