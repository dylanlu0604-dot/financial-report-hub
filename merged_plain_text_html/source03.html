With trillions of dollars, euros and yen changing hands every day, foreign

(geo)politics.

Introduction
The foreign exchange market is the world’s largest and most liquid financial market,
but it differs fundamentally from a centralised exchange. Trading takes place across
a fragmented, largely over-the-counter network of banks, non-bank liquidity
providers, electronic venues and end-users, using instruments ranging from spot
and forwards to swaps, futures and options. How do transactions move through

this network? Who provides liquidity, who consumes it and what risks arise
between execution and settlement? This chapter maps the FX ecosystem from
instruments and trading infrastructure to the participants ultimately responsible for
currency demand.
1.1 Instruments
The FX market is not one single product. Instead, it is made up of a group of
instruments that allow market participants to exchange currencies today, lock in an
exchange rate for the future and/or hedge against adverse exchange-rate moves.

Below, we outline the main instruments in FX markets and their users.
Spot: This is the simplest and most widely known FX instrument. When market
participants refer to “the exchange-rate” or the “spot exchange-rate,” they
generally mean the price agreed today for exchanging one currency for another on
the standard spot settlement date. For most major currency pairs spot settlement
is two business days later (T+2).1  For example, a spot purchase of EUR 1 million

against the USD at a EUR/USD exchange-rate of 1.20 means that the buyer receives
EUR 1 million in his or her bank account on the settlement date and the seller
receives USD 1.2 million from the buyer on the same date.

By convention, the first currency in an exchange-rate quotation is known as the
base currency, while the second is the quote currency. The exchange-rate therefore
represents the number of units of the quote currency required to purchase one unit
of the base currency. In USD/JPY, for example, the USD is the base currency and the
exchange-rate indicates the number of Japanese yen required to purchase one US

dollar. A USD/JPY exchange-rate of 160 means 1 USD can be exchanged for 160
JPY.
Spot transactions are the most commonly used foreign exchange payments for
cross-border payments for goods and services, the purchase of foreign financial
assets, as well as portfolio rebalancing and proprietary trading. In 2025, the Bank
for International Settlements (BIS) estimates spot turnover at $3trn per day,
accounting for 31% of global over-the-counter (OTC) FX turnover.

Forwards and futures: An (outright) forward is similar to a spot transaction, but is
instead an agreement to exchange two currencies at a rate (price) agreed today, but
with settlement taking place at a date more than T+2 days in the future. They are
also traded OTC. Forwards are most commonly used by those who have a known
future cash flow and want to remove uncertainty around the future exchange rate.
In 2025, the BIS estimates that they account for 19% of global turnover, or $1.8trn

per day.
Futures are economically similar to forwards but are standardised contracts traded
on exchanges and centrally cleared. This provides transparent pricing and reduces
bilateral counterparty risk, but offers less flexibility over contract size, maturity and
settlement terms than an OTC forward. Exchange-traded FX futures remain
considerably smaller than the OTC FX market.
Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs): An NDF is a cash-settled forward commonly

used for currencies where convertibility or offshore deliverability is restricted. Two
parties agree today on an exchange rate for a future date, but unlike a standard
forward, the underlying currencies are not exchanged. At maturity, the agreed NDF
rate is compared with a pre-specified fixing or reference rate. The difference is
usually settled in USD. NDFs are commonly used by corporates, banks and
investors to hedge or take exposure to currencies subject to capital controls.

Swaps: An FX swap combines two transactions: an exchange of currencies on an
initial date and a reverse exchange at a later date, with both exchange rates agreed
when the swap is initiated. They are used by institutions that hold cash in one
currency (e.g. EUR) but need funding in another (e.g. USD) for investment, hedging
or other needs. They are by far the largest FX instrument by turnover, with the BIS
estimating turnover of $4trn per day in April 2025, 42% of global FX turnover. A

swap is, in effect, a form of collateralised borrowing, with the collateral being cash
in a different currency to that of the currency borrowed.
Cross-currency (XCCY) swaps: A cross-currency swap is similar to an FX swap in
that principal amounts denominated in two different currencies are typically
exchanged at inception and reversed at maturity. The key difference is that a cross-
currency swap also involves periodic exchanges of interest payments denominated

in the two currencies over the life of the contract. These payments may be fixed or
floating. Cross-currency swaps therefore allow market participants to transform
both the currency denomination and, where relevant, the interest-rate
characteristics of an asset or liability.

Options: An option gives the holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell
one currency against another at a specified exchange rate during a specified period.
Options are used to hedge currency risk or speculate on currency movements and
are particularly useful when the end-user wants an asymmetric payoff profile.
Unlike a forward, an option can limit losses from an adverse currency movement
while allowing the holder to retain some or all of the benefit from a favourable

movement. In 2025, they represented around 7% of global FX turnover. Options are
widely used by corporates and investors for hedging, and by investors and dealers
to express directional or volatility views.
The simplest and most widely used instruments are vanilla options, which give the
holder the right to buy (a call) or sell (a put) a currency at a predetermined strike
price. A European-style option can be exercised only at expiry, while an American-

style option can be exercised at any point up to expiry. More structured products
include barrier options, whose payoff is activated or extinguished if the exchange
rate reaches a specified level—for example, knock-in and knock-out options. One-
touch and no-touch options pay a fixed amount depending on whether the
exchange-rate does, or does not, reach a predetermined level during the life of the
option. These instruments are commonly used to express views not just on the

direction of a currency pair, but also on the probability, timing and magnitude of
exchange-rate moves.

the broad steps involved in trading FX, focusing on spot transactions. They are:

a trade is executed, including client onboarding, platform selection, credit

particularly pronounced with forward contracts, where the longer pre-
settlement period leads to greater mark-to-market risk and, consequently,
higher settlement risk.
Market Risk: This risk in FX trading arises from potential losses due to adverse
exchange-rate movements. This is influenced by factors like economic data,

geopolitical events, and interest rates. For customers, asymmetric information,
where one party has more or better information than another, can lead to inefficient
pricing and increased volatility, thereby exacerbating market risk.
n For dealers, a key component of market risk is inventory management risk
– the potential for losses from unfavourable price movements in the
currencies they hold to facilitate client trading. Effective inventory
management, including position limits and hedging, mitigates this risk.

Liquidity Risk: This refers to the overall market's ability to facilitate the buying and
selling of a currency pair quickly and efficiently at a desired price. It's influenced by
factors such as market depth, trading volume, volatility, and fragmentation.
Operational Risk: This encompasses risks associated with trade processing,
settlement, and technology. Errors in trade execution, system failures, and fraud
can all lead to significant losses.
Regulatory Risk: This refers to the risk arising from changes in regulations or their

interpretation. The evolving regulatory landscape in FX, particularly concerning
non-bank dealers and systemic risk, can impact market participants and create
uncertainty.

1.5 The FX ecosystem
Following Grover and Saravelos (2025) , this section examines the significant

changes in FX market structure in recent decades, with a particular focus on the FX
spot market, and highlights the key challenges that have emerged as a result of this
transformation. To understand how these challenges emerged, it is useful to begin
by examining the evolution of the FX spot market.
1.5.1 The Evolution of FX Trading: From Voice to Electronic
We analyse the evolution of the FX spot market, focusing on the shift from a pre-
electronification era of voice-based trading and bank-centric risk management to

the phased electronification of interdealer and dealer-client segments. This
transformation has led to the emergence of diverse execution styles and trading
venues, which will be explored to provide a comprehensive overview of the
changing FX landscape and its implications for risk mitigation. The discussion
begins with an examination of the pre-electronification era.
1.Voice trading: The age of the telephone
Early foreign exchange (FX) trading was a two-tiered system conducted over the

phone (Figure 10). Clients contacted dealers (outer tier) directly to inquire about bid
and ask prices. Dealers profited from the difference between these prices (the bid-
ask spread). Dealers further traded amongst themselves (the "inter-dealer market"
- inner tier) to manage inventory imbalances arising from trading with clients. They
did this either directly or anonymously through voice brokers.
In this two-tier system, banks acted as the sole liquidity providers and risk bearers

in the FX market. Inter-dealer trading, used for hedging and inventory management,
formed a significant portion of overall trading volume.

n The introduction of electronic brokers, Reuters/Refinitiv (now LSEG) and

counterparty. Once the client executes the trade, it is “given up” to the prime broker.

matching trades: one between the executing dealer and the prime broker, and
another between the prime broker and the hedge fund client. The prime broker
consequently stands between the client and the wider dealer network from a credit
perspective, even though it need not be involved in choosing the price or executing
the original trade.

willing to establish a bilateral credit relationship with it, limiting the number of

1.5.3 Trading Venues: Anonymity and Liquidity
Different trading venues can be distinguished based on their offerings in terms of
two key characteristics: pre-trade anonymity and liquidity firmness (Chaboud et al.,
2023). Pre-trade anonymity refers to the degree to which counterparties remain
unknown to each other before a trade is executed. Liquidity firmness, on the other
hand, describes the certainty of execution at a quoted price.

Pre-trade anonymity: The level of anonymity can range from fully disclosed to fully
anonymous. Multi-dealer platforms (MDPs), acting as aggregators of dealer
liquidity, typically operate on a fully disclosed basis. Clients, both financial
institutions and corporations, submit requests for quote (RFQs) via the platform to
multiple dealers, revealing their identity before trade execution. Similarly, bank-
owned single-dealer platforms also function with full disclosure, as the bank dealer

knows the identity of the client submitting the RFQ.
At the other end, primary venues running a centralized limit order book (such as
EBS/Reuters) generally operate on a fully anonymous basis. The displayed orders
do not show a source and all participants can be matched based on the typical price
and time priority rules of a CLOB. However, it is important to note that unlike
exchanges, this still preserves bilateral relationship of an OTC market structure

Liquidity firmness: The firmness of liquidity in FX trading, meaning the certainty of
execution at a quoted price, is significantly influenced by the practice of "last look."
This practice allows liquidity providers (LPs) a brief window, typically milliseconds,

Last look was initially developed to address challenges posed by network latencies,

imposing additional hold times before trade confirmation.
Liquidity on primary venues is generally considered firm, as last look is prohibited.

Conversely, last look is widely employed on secondary venues and universally
available on MDPs and SDPs, where LPs retain the option to use it. Consequently,
liquidity on these platforms is viewed as less firm, introducing uncertainty
regarding trade execution upon hitting a quoted price.

The FX Liquidity Mirage: Myth or Reality?

The fragmentation of the FX market, driven by the decline of inter-dealer electronic
brokerage and the rise of diverse  trading venues, particularly in the dealer-to-
customer segment, presents significant challenges for liquidity assessment. The
proliferation of platforms can create a deceptive appearance of increased liquidity,
a "liquidity  mirage," as the  same liquidity  might be reflected across  multiple
interconnected venues.  Executing a trade on one platform  can trigger a

simultaneous reduction in liquidity  on others, leading to an overestimation  of
available liquidity when simply aggregating displayed market depth.
Further, accessing liquidity  across this fragmented landscape can be
technologically challenging and costly due to latency issues and access fees. While
aggregated depth  might suggest the  feasibility of executing  a large order,
attempting to do so could significantly impact prices as liquidity is tapped across

various platforms.
Accurately measuring FX liquidity requires a more sophisticated approach than
simply aggregating depth. Metrics like effective spreads (inclusive of slippage and
fees) and price impact offer a more realistic representation of transaction costs and
market depth. Analysing volume  distribution across venues can help identify
concentrations of genuine liquidity, but practical constraints such as technological
limitations and access fees must also be factored into the assessment.

Electronification of markets and flash crashes
The increasing attention given to flash crashes in recent years has coincided with
the electronification of financial markets. Computer algorithms are now used to
automatically make trading decisions, submit orders, and manage those orders
after submission. Algorithmic trading (AT) now contributes to more than 70% of
trading volumes in equity and FX markets  (Hendershott, 2012 and BIS, 2018).
Recent flash crashes have triggered an important policy debate over the merits of

algorithmic trading in financial markets. As argued in Deutsche Bank
research ( Grover et al., 2019), establishing a direct link between flash
crashes and electronification is far from straightforward. First, there is no

Introduction
Exchange rates are famously difficult to forecast. Why can currencies move sharply
when macroeconomic  fundamentals appear unchanged?  Why do apparently
compelling exchange-rate models so often fail out of sample? This chapter provides
an overview of FX drivers over the short, medium, and long-run horizon. We rely on
academic evidence, Deutsche Bank research and practitioner experience to show

that while there  is no single, universally  reliable model of exchange-rate
determination, there is a compelling set of frameworks and drivers that can
help explain and forecast exchange rate movements over time.
2.1 Why the efficient market hypothesis breaks down in FX
As the world’s largest and most liquid financial market, foreign exchange is often
regarded as a particularly strong candidate for satisfying the efficient market
hypothesis. This states that the current exchange rate fully reflects all publicly

available information and it should not be possible to use that information
systematically to earn abnormal risk-adjusted profits. In his seminal paper Fama
(1970) identified three conditions that would be sufficient to ensure market
efficiency:
(a)there are no transaction costs;
(b)all available information is freely available to all market participants; and
(c)all participants agree on the implications of that information for current
prices and the underlying model of the economy.

Omitted from these conditions is, however, a fourth and critically important one that
is only left implicit in Fama's paper:

### Page 31

The Currency Book
Deutsche Bank AG Page 31
(d) a sufficient number of market participants are profit-seeking and are able
to move the market rapidly to equilibrium valuations.
FX market structure itself provides perhaps the most compelling reason why the
mechanism supporting market efficiency in FX may be significantly weaker than is
commonly assumed. Earlier in this book we presented evidence from several sources
suggesting that FX turnover is divided roughly evenly between transactions

motivated by profit and transactions undertaken for commercial, hedging,
institutional or policy purposes. Moreover, with the expansion of passive investment
and the increasing importance of regulatory, mandate and balance sheet
constraints, the relative importance of unconstrained, return-seeking risk capital
may be declining.
A high share of non-profit-motivated or price-insensitive demand has important
implications. If transactional flows are large relative to the capital available to

arbitrageurs, prices may then reflect not only expectations of future fundamentals,
but also hedging demand, corporate cash flows, reserve-management decisions,
benchmark rebalancing, regulation and the balance sheet capacity of
intermediaries. These forces will generate persistent deviations from conventional
measures of fundamental value and predictable returns as compensation for
providing liquidity and bearing inventory or balance sheet risk.

Note that the market inefficiency argument does not require non-speculative
participants to be irrational or poorly informed. It rests on the simpler observation
that, unlike equities and bonds, currencies are required to complete a broad range of
underlying economic transactions unrelated to FX profit-seeking. Other
participants may seek to maximise returns but be constrained by leverage limits,
risk budgets, benchmarks, regulation or investment mandates. The presence of

such participants weakens the practical arbitrage mechanism through which
information is presumed to be incorporated into prices. Where non-profit-
motivated flows are large and the capital of informed speculators is limited,
exchange-rate determination is likely to depend on a broader and more distinctive
set of forces than in traditional asset markets.
2.2 Underlying principles of exchange rate determination
First, like all asset prices, the exchange rate is forward-looking: today's level

embeds expectations about the entire future path of fundamentals and of risk
premia. Engel and West (2005) formalised this, showing that in a rational-
expectations present-value model when fundamentals are shifting over time the
exchange rate will behave almost exactly like a random walk under certain
conditions. This is not because fundamentals are irrelevant, but because the
exchange rate optimally aggregates expected future fundamentals. This reconciles

the empirical failure of fundamentals to forecast currencies with the belief that
currencies are ultimately driven by fundamentals.
Second, exchange rates are not purely driven by macroeconomic but also financial
drivers. In the traditional academic paradigm, the exchange rate equilibrated goods
and money markets. In the modern paradigm, it is set at the margin by financial
intermediaries and investors operating in imperfect, segmented capital markets

subject to balance sheet constraints and time-varying risk-bearing capacity. This
shift, associated with Gabaix and Maggiori (2015), Itskhoki and Mukhin (2021),
Jiang, Krishnamurthy and Lustig (2021) and the broader intermediary-asset-pricing
literature explains not only long-standing "puzzles" of exchange-rate determination
but also begins to explain phenomena like the cross-currency basis.

2.3 Time horizons of exchange rate determination
Predicting exchange rates is famously difficult. A helpful organising principle, and
the one that structures the remainder of this chapter, is to think about exchange rate
determination across three horizons:
n In the short run (intraday to a few weeks), exchange rates are dominated by
order flow, positioning, sentiment, risk appetite, data releases and
technical/trend-following behaviour.

n In the medium run (months to a couple of years), cyclical macro forces —
interest rate differentials, the monetary policy cycle, relative growth, the
current account and capital flows pull currencies around their long-run
anchors.
n In the long run (several years and beyond), fundamentals reassert
themselves: relative prices (PPP), productivity (Balassa-Samuelson), terms
of trade, and net international investment positions shape the equilibrium

real exchange rate.
In the next few sections we will proceed by horizon and by mechanism. We begin
with the long-run anchors (PPP, productivity, terms of trade, external balances and
equilibrium-exchange-rate concepts), then turn to the medium-run parity
conditions and macro models, then to the short-run microstructure of the market
(order flow, positioning, options and technical analysis). We then review
forecasting performance tests, before moving on to modern financial theories of

the exchange rate, central bank intervention, currency regimes and crises, and
finally a set of frontier topics — machine learning and digital currencies.

The PPP puzzle and the half-life debate
The central empirical problem, crystallised by Rogoff (1996) as the "PPP puzzle", is

FX deviations from PPP are highly persistent and volatile. In Rogoff's (1996, p. 648)
own words, "consensus estimates for the rate at which PPP deviations damp...
suggest a half-life of three to five years, seemingly far too long to be explained by
nominal rigidities." Such slow reversion is very hard to reconcile with nominal price
stickiness that lasts one to two years; something other than sticky goods prices
must be driving the persistence.
The survey by Taylor and Taylor (2004, Journal of Economic Perspectives)

documents how the profession's view on PPP has evolved, concluding that by the
early 2000s "the idea of long-run PPP now enjoys perhaps its strongest support in
more than thirty years." Two methodological advances drove this:
1.  Panel and long-horizon data greatly increased the power to reject the
random-walk hypothesis and detect mean reversion.
2.  More importantly, nonlinear mean reversion modelling is used: because
arbitrage in goods markets is subject to transaction costs, trade barriers

and large non-traded sectors, the real exchange rate reverts faster the
further it is from equilibrium, and can wander almost freely within a "band
of inaction". Once nonlinearity is modelled, estimated half-lives fall and
PPP reversion looks far more consistent with economic theory. The more
recent survey by Vo and Vo (2023, Journal of Economic Surveys) confirms
the long-run validity of PPP and a 3-5 year half-life.

2.5 Productivity, the Balassa-Samuelson effect and terms of trade
Balassa-Samuelson
Absolute PPP based on broad consumer-price baskets fails partly because those
baskets include large non-traded components (services, housing). These
components cannot be arbitraged over  borders. The Balassa-Samuelson (BS)
hypothesis provides the classic explanation for systematic trend deviations from
absolute PPP.
The logic is that countries that experience faster productivity growth in their traded-

goods sector can pay higher wages there; wage equalisation across sectors pushes
up wages, and hence prices, in the non-traded sector; this raises the country's
overall price level and appreciates its real exchange rate. Rich, high productivity
countries therefore have higher price levels and currencies that look "overvalued"
on a naive  PPP metric but are in fact  in equilibrium. Productivity convergence
should thus produce trend real appreciation in catching-up economies, or even in

developed economies with different productivity rates.
The empirical record on Balassa-Samuelson (BS) is mixed.  Egert, Halpern and
MacDonald (2006) and the survey by Tica and Druzic (2006) document that the
hypothesis has been tested dozens of times across scores of countries. They find
that the evidence is strongest for the intermediate links in the mechanism: richer or
more productive economies have higher relative prices, and faster tradable-sector

productivity growth is often associated with  rising non-tradable prices. Cross-
sectional tests are overwhelmingly supportive, while panel and time-series results
are more heterogeneous and sensitive to the countries, periods and econometric
specifications used.
Evidence for the full proposition, that productivity differentials reliably
explain aggregate real  exchange-rate appreciation, is considerably weaker,
in part because purchasing-power parity often fails even within the

tradable sector. Productivity catch-up tends to raise relative service prices and
can generate real appreciation, but its magnitude is highly uncertain and it is
rarely sufficient on its own to explain observed exchange-rate movements.
Terms of trade and commodity currencies
For commodity-exporting economies, the  terms of trade, the  price of exports
relative to imports, can be a more important driver of the real exchange rate than
productivity. A rise in the  world price of a country's main export  commodity

increases the purchasing power of its exports  and raises national  income and
wealth. Higher income increases domestic  demand, including for non-tradable
goods and services whose prices are determined locally. Because the prices of
internationally traded goods are more tightly  anchored by world markets, the
resulting rise in the relative price of non-tradables produces a real exchange-rate
appreciation.
This mechanism is sometimes associated with what is known as "Dutch disease".

In the classic framework, a commodity boom generates a spending  effect, as
higher national income raises demand and prices in the non-traded sector, and
potentially a resource-movement effect, as labour and capital shift toward  the
booming commodity  sector and away  from other tradable industries. Both

exchange rate. The resulting currency appreciation can in turn reduce the

2.6 External balances, net foreign assets and equilibrium exchange rate

The flow and stock approaches
Two related traditions link the exchange rate to a country's external position. The
flow (current account) approach holds that a currency must adjust to bring the
current account toward a sustainable level: persistent deficits require a weaker real
exchange rate to restore competitiveness. The stock (portfolio/net-foreign-asset)

approach emphasies that a country running deficits accumulates external
liabilities, and the real exchange rate must eventually be weak enough to generate
the trade surpluses that service them or strong enough, for creditor nations, to
permit the running down of surpluses. The policy and sell-side community
operationalises these ideas through equilibrium exchange-rate models, of which
there are three main families:
n The FEER (Fundamental Equilibrium Exchange Rate), (Williamson 1994)

and the closely related DEER (Desired EER) are normative,
macroeconomic-balance concepts: they identify the real effective
exchange rate consistent with the economy achieving internal balance (full
employment, target inflation) and external balance (a "sustainable" current
account) over the medium term. FEER requires an assumption about a
current account equilibrium and trade elasticities but is largely model-free
about the adjustment path. Such an approach is used as part of our FX

Valuation Snapshot.
n The BEER (Behavioural Equilibrium Exchange Rate), Clark and
MacDonald 1998) is essentially an econometric model. It regresses the
observed real exchange rate on a set of fundamentals, net foreign assets,
productivity/relative prices (Balassa-Samuelson), the terms of trade, and
real interest-rate differentials, and defines the equilibrium as the fitted
value, with the residual measuring misalignment. One of our proprietary
valuation frameworks use a BEER model, called DBeer. It uses terms of

trade, a productivity ratio and trade openness. Grover (2025) shows it
produces mean-reversion signals and that, in contrast to PPP, DBeer
performs better in emerging markets, where idiosyncratic and terms-of-
trade dynamics dominate, while PPP dominates in G10.
n The IMF’s External Balance Assessment (EBA), introduced in 2012, is not
a separate exchange rate theory so much as a framework that combines
three approaches. First, its principal current account model estimates an

underlying current account balance and compares it with a “norm”
consistent with economic fundamentals and policies set at their desired
levels. The resulting current account gap is then converted, using estimated
trade elasticities, into the real-exchange-rate adjustment that would be
required to close it. In this respect, the operational procedure is similar to a
FEER calculation, although the current-account norm is itself estimated
econometrically rather than imposed entirely outside the model. Second,

EBA also contains direct REER panel regressions that are closer in spirit to
the BEER approach, estimating the exchange rate implied by fundamentals
and desired policy settings. A third, external-sustainability approach
calculates the current-account balance needed to stabilise or move the net
foreign asset position towards an appropriate benchmark. IMF staff use the
three approaches as cross-checks and combine them with country-specific
evidence and judgement to arrive at an overall assessment of the current-

account and REER gaps.

2.7 Medium-run macro models
The monetary model of exchange rates

The monetary approach treats the exchange rate as the relative price of two monies,
determined by money supplies, output (money demand) and interest rates. In
Dornbusch's (1976) sticky-price version this is how exchange rate overshooting is
generated. Because goods prices are sticky, a monetary expansion causes the
exchange rate to depreciate more than proportionately on impact before reverting,
explaining why exchange rates are more volatile than the underlying fundamentals.

The original monetary model dominated academic thinking in the 1970s but was
challenged by the forecasting failure documented by Meese and Rogoff (1983). It
nonetheless remains a useful framework for thinking about the exchange rate
consequences of relative monetary policy and the interest rate differential is a key
input used by FX market practitioners to this day.
Covered and uncovered interest parity, carry trades in FX
The relationship between interest rate differentials and exchange rates is one of the

most widely studied in academic research and has given birth to what are known
as the two "parity conditions".
n Covered interest parity (CIP) is an arbitrage relationship linking spot and
forward exchange rates to interest rates across currencies. It states that an
investor should earn the same return from investing in domestic cash as
from converting funds into a foreign currency, investing at the foreign
interest rate, and simultaneously using a forward contract to lock in the

exchange rate at which the proceeds will be converted back. If the two
hedged returns differed, investors could borrow in the lower-returning
market and invest in the higher-returning one, using the forward market to
eliminate exchange-rate risk, until the arbitrage opportunity disappeared.

### Page 39

The Currency Book
Deutsche Bank AG Page 39
CIP held almost exactly in the data until 2008 (see later section).
n Uncovered interest parity (UIP) is a looser condition: it says the expected
change in the exchange rate should equal the interest rate differential, so
that a high-interest-rate currency should be expected to depreciate by
exactly enough to offset its yield advantage. UIP is tested through the Fama
(1984) regression of realised exchange-rate changes on the interest

differential (or forward premium):
Δs = α + β(i − i*) + ε
Where Δs is the change in the base currency and i is the base-currency interest rate.
UIP predicts β = 1. The overwhelming empirical finding for decades was that the
estimated β was not only below one but frequently negative. This is what is known
as the forward premium puzzle in academia or as the "carry trade" in daily market
practice: high-yield currencies tend, on average, to appreciate rather than

depreciate, which is exactly what makes the carry trade profitable. This is one of the
most robust anomalies in international finance.

Taylor-rule fundamentals and interest rate differentials
In its most recent form, monetary modelling of exchange rates replaces money
supplies with the monetary policy reaction function and interest rates. If central
banks set interest rates according to a Taylor rule (responding to inflation and the
output gap), then the interest differential and hence, via UIP, the expected exchange
rate change can be expressed in terms of relative inflation and output gaps. Engel

and West (2005), Molodtsova and Papell (2009) and Rossi (2013) showed that
Taylor-rule fundamentals can help model exchange rates. With Rossi finding that
Taylor-rule and net-foreign-asset fundamentals as the most promising predictors.
The strong academic support of monetary exchange-rate modelling is reflected in
the strong emphasis on interest rate differentials in market practitioner analysis.
Relative interest rates remain among the most closely watched drivers of exchange

rates among market participants, but the distinction between nominal and real
differentials is critical:
n Nominal interest-rate differentials determine the carry earned on an
unhedged currency position and therefore matter directly for short-run
portfolio allocation; in practice, currencies often respond most strongly to
revisions in the expected path of policy rates rather than to the current
policy rate itself.
n For medium-run valuation, however, the more relevant concept is the

expected ex ante real interest-rate differential, the nominal rate adjusted for
expected inflation, because it measures the relative return on purchasing
power. A high nominal yield generated by tighter monetary policy and a
higher real rate should, other things equal, support a currency, whereas the
same nominal yield caused by high expected inflation may provide little
support and can instead be associated with depreciation.
This distinction is central to Frankel’s (1979) real-interest-differential model,

although the empirical relationship is not stable. Meese and Rogoff (1988) find little
robust connection between real exchange rates and real-rate differentials, while
Baxter (1994) found a stronger relationship at business-cycle and lower
frequencies. The practical conclusion is that nominal differentials matter most for
carry and near-term policy pricing, while expected real rate differentials are the
more meaningful, though still imperfect, guide to medium-run exchange-rate

valuation.

Current accounts, capital flows and the basic balance
Current accounts provide one of the clearest medium-run anchors for exchange
rates because they summarise the gap between national saving and domestic
investment. Persistent current-account deficits imply that domestic consumption
exceeds output and must be financed by net capital inflows, while persistent

surpluses imply the opposite. In the medium run, those external positions cannot
drift without consequence: a weaker net foreign asset position must eventually be
stabilised through stronger future net exports, favourable valuation effects on the
external portfolio, or both, and the exchange rate is one of the key relative prices
through which that adjustment is delivered (Chinn and Prasad, 2003; Gourinchas
and Rey, 2007).
The composition of capital flows matters as much as the headline balance. Portfolio

equity and bond flows are typically more sensitive to relative returns, hedging
demand and global risk appetite, so marginal reallocations in these categories can
produce sizeable currency moves. FDI is usually the stickier component of external
financing, reflecting the partial illiquidity of direct ownership. For FX, a deficit
financed by long-horizon FDI is therefore very different from one financed by return-
seeking portfolio debt or equity inflows that can reverse quickly when relative yields

or risk sentiment change (Hau and Rey, 2006; Albuquerque, 2003; Gabaix and
Maggiori, 2015; Greenwood et al., 2023).
Large changes in the trading regime can also shift the expected path of the external
balance abruptly. Events such as Brexit, the imposition of tariffs or a major trade
agreement can alter expected export demand, import costs, investment and the
location of production, with potentially significant consequences for the
equilibrium exchange rate. Importantly, currencies are forward-looking asset

prices: they need not wait for a trade shock to appear in realised current account
data. Once markets assign a sufficiently high probability to a future change in trade
arrangements, the exchange rate can adjust immediately in anticipation of its
expected effects on future trade flows, investment and national income. The
sizeable adjustment in sterling around the 2016 Brexit referendum is a prominent
example of the exchange rate moving well before the full effects of the new trading

relationship became visible in the balance of payments data.

The importance of hedge ratios as a medium-run FX driver
Alongside the current account and portfolio flow channels discussed above, the

hedging decision on existing foreign asset stocks may become a first-order driver
of exchange rates. The mechanism is a stock adjustment one: when a non-US
pension fund or insurer holds unhedged US securities, a decision to raise its hedge
ratio does not require selling the underlying bond or equity, but it does generate a
flow of dollar sales to enact the forward hedge that is then rolled in perpetuity.
Because the pool of cross-border foreign ownership of assets is very large, even

modest shifts in the desired aggregate hedge ratios of foreign investors can
dominate other flows in the balance of payments. The medium-run character of the
channel comes from its persistence: hedge ratios are sticky, adjusted slowly and in
cycles rather than day-to-day.
The incentive to hedge is governed largely by the cost of hedging, which for a rolling
short-dated hedge would tend to be in the 3m to 1-year tenor and approximated by
the front-end interest rate differential plus the cross-currency basis. A wide positive

rate differential between the US and the rest of the world makes selling the dollar
forward expensive, it subtracts directly from the hedged return and, when the
hedged yield on a US bond turns negative, removes the entire rationale for holding
the asset in hedged form. This is precisely why hedge ratios fell across the
developed world as US front-end rates rose after 2022, leaving investors
structurally under-hedged and dollar exposure near historical highs (Gopal, Baker

and Dynan, 2025).
The relationship is not mechanical, however and after the US administration's trade
actions in 2025 investors raised dollar hedges sharply in response to surprise shifts
in US economic and foreign policy (Shin, Wooldridge and Xia, 2025 and Saravelos,
2025). The practical conclusion is that hedge ratio dynamics are best read as a joint
function of the short-end rate differential (the cost) and broader shifts in underlying
currency views (Grover and Harvey, 2024).

2.8 Short-run determination I: Order flow and market microstructure
Order flow and market microstructure
Because macro fundamentals are near-useless at high frequency, the short-run
behaviour of exchange rates is the domain of market microstructure, the study of
how the trading process itself conveys information and moves prices. The initial
insight, due to Evans and Lyons (2002), is that order flow (signed transaction

volume — the net of buyer-initiated and seller-initiated trades) is the proximate
driver of exchange rates and the channel through which dispersed information
about fundamentals, risk appetite and hedging demand is aggregated into price.
Order flow explains a large share of daily and intraday exchange-rate variation,
more than macro news alone.
The most important modern contribution is Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling and
Schrimpf (2016), where using a large dealer's disaggregated customer order flow,

they show that order flow is highly informative about future exchange rates.
Crucially, different customer segments (asset managers, hedge funds, corporates,
private clients) differ markedly in their trading styles, information content and risk
exposures. Financial and non-financial customers tend to trade in opposite
directions, so the dealers intermediate risk sharing between them, and the
information in financial-customer flow has a persistent, longer-term impact on

returns consistent with it carrying fundamental information.

Deutsche Bank's CORAX indicators (Categorised ORderflow from Autobahn FX),

literature to be applied directly. Grover (2021), building explicitly on Menkhoff et al.
(2016), studies discretionary versus systematic hedge-fund flows across G10 and
liquid EM from January 2016. The findings largely mirror the results of the academic

2.9 Short-run determination II: Positioning, options flow, sentiment, data
releases and technical analysis
Positioning
Because currencies can overshoot and trend, measures of market positioning are
valuable, particularly as contrarian signals at extremes. Positioning refers to the
stock of existing long and short currency exposures held by investors: a market is

described as “long” a currency when investors have, in aggregate, built positions
that benefit from its appreciation, and “short” when they are positioned for
depreciation. This differs from flows, which measure new buying and selling over
a period; positioning instead captures the cumulative result of past flows and
therefore indicates how much directional risk is already embedded in investor
portfolios.
Positioning matters because the impact of new information depends partly on how

investors are already positioned. When a currency has been rising for some time,
investors may progressively add to long positions, reinforcing the trend. But once
positioning becomes crowded, fewer investors remain to initiate additional longs,
while a negative surprise can force existing holders to unwind positions
simultaneously. Stop-losses, margin constraints and systematic trading strategies
can amplify this process, turning an initially modest reversal into a sharp move.

Conversely, an extremely short currency can rally strongly even on modestly
positive news as investors rush to cover their positions. Positioning is therefore
most useful not as a mechanical timing indicator, but as a measure of market
vulnerability and asymmetry: stretched positioning raises the probability that an
adverse surprise generates a disproportionately large price response.

Given the size and depth of the currency option market, option prices can contain
information about market expectations and risk sentiment that is not fully visible in
spot or forward rates. Implied volatility measures the market price of protection
against exchange rate movements, while the volatility smile provides information
about the perceived balance of tail risks.

The most widely followed measure of this asymmetry is the risk reversal, usually
calculated as the implied volatility of an out-of-the-money currency call minus that
of an equivalent-delta currency put. A positive risk reversal means that calls on the
base currency are more expensive than comparable puts, indicating greater

demand for protection against, or exposure to, base-currency appreciation. Risk
reversals should not, however, be interpreted as pure forecasts: they reflect the
market’s risk-neutral distribution and therefore combine expected outcomes with
risk premia, hedging demand and position imbalances. More generally, option
prices can be used to recover information about the market-implied distribution of
future exchange rates, including its volatility, skewness and tail probabilities

(Campa, Chang and Reider, 1998).
Options can also affect the exchange rate mechanically through the hedging
activity they generate. Dealers typically manage the delta of their option books (the
change to the value of their positions driven by spot exchange-rate movements) by
buying or selling the underlying currency as spot moves. The direction of these
flows depends importantly on the market’s aggregate gamma, which measures
how rapidly an option portfolio’s delta changes with the exchange rate. Dealers

who are long gamma generally sell the currency after it rises and buy it after it falls,
creating countercyclical flows that can dampen volatility and sometimes pull spot
towards large strikes around expiry. Dealers who are short gamma must instead
buy as the currency rises and sell as it falls, producing procyclical flows that can
amplify an existing move. Empirical evidence using trade-repository data confirms
that negative market-maker gamma can increase spot-market volatility, while

positive gamma has the opposite effect (Anderegg, Ulmann and Sornette, 2022).
These effects can become especially pronounced around barrier options, which are
activated or extinguished when the exchange rate reaches a predetermined level.
As spot approaches a barrier, the option’s delta and gamma may change sharply,
requiring increasingly large hedge adjustments. Investors with an interest in
preventing the barrier from being triggered may also trade defensively around the

level. If the barrier is ultimately breached, the associated option exposure and
hedges can disappear or change abruptly, generating a sudden reversal of previous
hedging flows or accelerating the move through the level. The precise direction
depends on whether the option is a knock-in or knock-out, whether it is a call or put,

Technical analysis and trend-following
Technical analysis refers to forecasting future price movements from patterns in
past market data rather than from an explicit model of economic fundamentals. In
FX, this typically means analysing the history of exchange rates and sometimes

volume, volatility or positioning to identify trends, momentum, turning points and
important price levels.
Common techniques include moving averages, which generate signals when a
short-term price trend crosses a longer-term one; momentum and oscillator
indicators, which attempt to identify overbought or oversold conditions; and
support and resistance levels, where previous highs, lows or other widely watched
prices are expected to influence future trading behaviour. More sophisticated

systematic versions of technical analysis translate these ideas into mechanical
trend-following or breakout rules.

The underlying premise is not necessarily that past prices contain fundamental

prices. Technical analysis is pervasive in FX markets, with surveys finding
widespread use among FX dealers and professional fund managers, associated

with the belief that psychological and non-fundamental factors can influence prices

There is also a substantial literature showing that simple technical trading rules
have historically generated statistically and economically significant returns in FX.
Levich and Thomas (1993), using bootstrap tests on currency futures, find that
moving-average and filter rules produced profits that were difficult to reconcile with

simple random exchange-rate processes. Neely, Weller and Dittmar (1997)
similarly find significant out-of-sample excess returns across several major
exchange rates using rules selected by genetic-programming techniques.

explanations for the use of technical analysis: irrational behaviour, exploitation of
central bank intervention, efficient processing of dispersed information, and the

ability of technical signals to capture non-fundamental influences on exchange
rates. They regard the final explanation as the most persuasive. In this
interpretation, technical analysis is useful because FX prices can be driven for
extended periods by order flow, positioning, herding and other market dynamics
that are not immediately captured by standard macroeconomic models.

Risk sentiment and impact on FX
Risk sentiment is an important cross-cutting driver of exchange rates because it
changes investors’ willingness and capacity to hold leveraged or risky currency
positions. During periods of strong risk appetite and low volatility, investors tend to
borrow in low-yielding funding currencies and invest in higher-yielding currencies,
supporting carry-trade returns. When volatility  rises or market  liquidity

deteriorates, however, losses, tighter funding constraints and margin calls  can
trigger a simultaneous unwinding of these positions: high-yielding and relatively
illiquid currencies are sold, while funding currencies are repurchased, amplifying
the initial exchange-rate move.
Brunnermeier, Nagel and Pedersen  (2009) document the  resulting negative
skewness and crash risk of carry trades, while Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling and
Schrimpf (2012) show  that high-yield currencies perform  poorly following

increases in global FX volatility. Risk appetite is generally perceived to be negatively
correlated with the broad US dollar: the dollar often strengthens when global risk
sentiment deteriorates, reflecting its central role in international funding, liquidity
and safe-asset demand. The relationship between the dollar and risk appetite is
however not stable over time, with shocks that disproportionately impact the
US such as the bursting of the 2000 dot-com bubble often associated with a

weaker dollar.
2.10 Forecasting performance and the Meese-Rogoff Puzzle
Meese and Rogoff (1983), showed that the structural exchange-rate models of the
day, monetary, portfolio-balance, could not out-forecast a naive random walk out
of sample, even when fed the realised (future) values of the fundamentals. Four
decades on, the Meese-Rogoff puzzle remains the benchmark against which every
forecasting claim is measured, and the  random walk without drift has proven

extraordinarily hard to beat at horizons under a year.
One of the  most useful modern surveys  on exchange-rate forecasting is Rossi
(2013, Journal of Economic Literature). Her review  of the post-1983 literature
reaches a mixed verdict: predictability does exist, but it is fragile and conditional.
Parameter instability is one of the biggest challenges to systematic exchange-rate
modelling: it is not only that fundamentals change over time, but the relationship

between these and exchange rates changes too.

2.11 Modern theories of the exchange rate
Exchange rates dominated by financial markets
With financial markets outpacing the real economy in size and growth, Gabaix and
Maggiori (2015) provide a theory in which exchange rates are determined by capital
flows intermediated through imperfect financial markets. Gabaix and Maggiori
introduce financial intermediaries (primarily banks) with limited risk-bearing

capacity into their model. When, for example, investors in one country increase
their demand for foreign assets, intermediaries must take the opposite currency
exposure to clear the market. As their balance sheets become more exposed, they
require greater compensation for bearing that risk, and the exchange rate moves
sufficiently to induce them to absorb the imbalance. The larger the required flow
relative to intermediaries' risk-bearing capacity, the larger the resulting exchange-

rate adjustment. This provides an important explanation for why exchange rates
can move substantially even when trade flows or conventional macroeconomic
fundamentals change little. Shifts in international portfolio preferences, financial
regulation, intermediary balance sheet capacity or global risk appetite can all
generate exchange-rate movements by changing the willingness of financial
institutions to intermediate the resulting flows.
The framework is therefore particularly useful for understanding episodes such as

the 2008 global financial crisis, when large exchange-rate movements were
associated with deleveraging, balance-sheet constraints cross-border portfolio
adjustment rather than simply changes in goods trade. A closely related
perspective is provided by Rey (2015), who argues that the US dollar sits at the core
of the global financial cycle. The large pool of dollars sitting outside the US banking
system means easier US financial conditions are associated with expanding cross-

border leverage and capital flows and, typically, a weaker dollar. Conversely, tighter
financial conditions and rising global risk aversion trigger deleveraging,
repatriation and demand for dollar liquidity, tending to strengthen the dollar. The
dollar therefore acts not simply as the relative price of US and foreign goods, but as
a key price through which changes in global financial conditions and risk-bearing
capacity are transmitted internationally.

Conventional PPP and real effective exchange rate measures weight currencies
according to bilateral trade, implicitly reflecting the importance of different
countries in goods and services markets. This financial-market perspective is the
motivation behind the Deutsche Bank's Capital-weighted PPP model. A capital-
weighted approach instead gives greater weight to currencies against which a
country has large cross-border financial exposures, recognising that the relevant

counterparties for exchange rate determination may increasingly be determined by
the geography of portfolios rather than trade. Our capital-based valuation
measures therefore construct a real effective financial exchange rate using capital
weights and compare its level with a historical equilibrium in much the same way
that conventional PPP compares a trade-weighted real exchange rate with its long-
run norm. For countries with very large external asset and liability positions, this can

yield materially different measures of currency valuation from traditional trade-
weighted PPP and provides a useful complement in a world where gross capital
flows substantially exceed the flows associated with international trade.

Convenience yields and safe-asset demand for dollars
A complementary financial mechanism, specific to the dollar, is the convenience
yield on safe dollar assets. Jiang, Krishnamurthy and Lustig (2021) develop a theory
in which the dollar's value reflects the convenience (safety and liquidity) services

that global investors derive from holding US Treasuries. This convenience yield is
measurable via the Treasury basis (the yield gap between US government bonds
and currency-hedged foreign government bonds); when it widens, the dollar
appreciates immediately and then depreciates subsequently. In their words, "the
variation in the Treasury basis accounts for 25 percent of the quarterly variation in
the dollar between 1988 and 2017," and "on average, foreign investors earn an extra

convenience yield of 2% on Treasury holdings," 90% of which is directly attributable
to the dollar denomination.

The Dominant Currency Paradigm and Invoicing

The Dominant Currency Paradigm (DCP) of Gopinath, Boz, Casas, Díez, Gourinchas
and Plagborg-Møller (2020) and alters the textbook framework in which prices are
set either in the producer's currency or the destination's. Using a dataset of more
than 2,500 country pairs covering 91% of world trade, they show that the vast
majority of world trade is invoiced in a small number of dominant currencies,
overwhelmingly the US dollar, and that those invoice prices are sticky in dollars

(Sachdeva, 2026).
This has important implications: exchange rate movements against the dollar,
rather than bilateral rates, drive trade prices and quantities and expenditure
switching operates weakly and asymmetrically. The dollar therefore exerts a
systemic influence on global trade and inflation that no other currency matches,
reinforcing the demand for dollar invoicing, dollar funding and dollar reserves in a
self-sustaining equilibrium. Combined with the safe-asset/convenience-yield

2.16 Bringing it all together
No single model of exchange rate determination is right all the time; the challenge
lies in matching the tool to the horizon and the regime. The evidence assembled
here supports a staged approach to exchange rate determination.
n First, anchor exchange rates with valuation (strategic, 3 years+). Use PPP,
BEER and FEER to establish a fair-value range and the direction of long-run
misalignment. Prefer PPP for G10 and DBeer for EM.

n Second, align FX forecasting with the economic cycle (cyclical, 1–2 years).
Use interest rate differentials, both level (carry) and change, the monetary
policy cycle and Taylor rule fundamentals for medium-run direction. Take
account of shifts in the balance of payments, both on the current account
and the capital flow side of the ledger.
n Third, flow and positioning matter (tactical overlay). Use order flow, options
flow and positioning as high-frequency short-horizon mean-reversion

signals.
n Finally, be aware of regime changes. This can take the form of a change in
exchange rate regime from a peg to a float, an underlying abrupt shift in
macroeconomic fundamentals (Brexit) or a large financial shock that shifts
the market to a new equilibrium (2008 Financial Crisis or Eurozone crisis).

Introduction
Currency investing starts from a different premise to equities or bonds: a currency
has no stand-alone expected return and every exposure is inherently relative,
involving a long position in one currency and a short position in another, typically
implemented through spot, forwards or options. Do investors make money from
trading FX? Can FX be treated as an investable asset class? Our first chapter

demonstrated the importance of non-profit-seeking participants in the FX market.
This weakens the application of the efficient-market hypothesis and creates scope
for excess returns. Our second chapter introduced the exchange-rate frameworks
that can inform an investment approach to currencies. This chapter turns from
theory to real-world evidence. We examine whether systematic currency factors
such as carry, momentum and value have generated persistent returns, whether

quantitative investors can capture them after costs and whether discretionary
currency managers generate alpha beyond these systematic exposures.
3.1 FX "beta" and factor investing
The poor out-of-sample performance of models designed to forecast exchange rate
levels, together with the persistent empirical rejection of uncovered interest parity
(UIP) has generated a large investor ecosystem focused on harvesting currency risk
premia via systematic strategies and indices across baskets of currencies rather

than individual exchange rates. Three investment-style factors have become
canonical:
n Carry involves buying high-interest-rate currencies and funding the
position by selling low-interest-rate currencies. Its profitability is the
investment counterpart of the forward-premium puzzle: high-yielding
currencies have not, on average, depreciated sufficiently to eliminate their
interest-rate advantage. Carry returns are often interpreted as
compensation for exposure to global risk, since high-yielding currencies

### Page 60

The Currency Book
Page 60 Deutsche Bank AG
tend to underperform during episodes of rising volatility, deteriorating
liquidity and widespread deleveraging (Lustig, Roussanov and Verdelhan,
2011; Menkhoff et al., 2012a).
n Momentum involves buying currencies with strong recent excess returns—
the “winners”—and selling recent “losers.” Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling
and Schrimpf (2012b) document substantial cross-sectional momentum in
currencies. Proposed explanations include the gradual incorporation of

information into exchange rates, investor underreaction and overreaction,
and limits to arbitrage, although transaction costs reduce the returns
available to implementable strategies.
n Value, sometimes described as a valuation or mean-reversion strategy,
involves buying currencies judged to be undervalued and selling those
judged to be overvalued relative to an equilibrium benchmark such as
purchasing-power parity or a behavioural equilibrium exchange rate

model. Value signals generally operate over longer horizons than carry or
momentum, reflecting the slow tendency of real exchange rates to revert
towards equilibrium. Evidence for currency value strategies forms part of
the broader finding that value and momentum premia appear across
several asset classes (Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen, 2013). Practitioner
implementations include Deutsche Bank’s PPP and DBeer frameworks
(Grover, 2025).
One of the earliest practical attempts to package these systematic returns into a

currency “beta” equivalent to the S&P 500 for equities was the Deutsche Bank
Currency Returns (DBCR) Index, introduced in 2007. The index equally weights
transparent, rules-based carry, momentum and valuation strategies across liquid
developed-market currencies and was designed to be investable after transaction
costs. In its original backtest over 1980–2006, DBCR generated an annualised
excess return of approximately 4 per cent, volatility of 5.2 per cent and a Sharpe ratio

of 0.77, with a maximum drawdown of 11 per cent. Its relatively low correlations
with global bonds and equities illustrated the potential diversification benefits of
combining systematic currency exposure with traditional asset classes (Hafeez,
2007).
A subsequent reassessment by Saravelos et al. (2018) found that the risk-adjusted
returns of the combined carry, momentum and value approach had remained
resilient after the global financial crisis. Over the longer sample beginning in the

1980s, systematic currency returns were found to be comparable on a risk-adjusted
basis with those of global bond and equity portfolios, while adding FX exposure to
a conventional portfolio reduced historical volatility and drawdowns.
These benefits arise partly because the component factors perform well in different
environments: momentum can offset some of the losses suffered by carry and
value during sharp regime changes, while valuation may benefit from the eventual

reversal of prolonged trends.
Factor returns nevertheless remain cyclical and can be exposed to crash risk,
crowding, changing monetary regimes and implementation costs; they should
therefore be understood as compensation for bearing a collection of risks rather
than as mechanical arbitrage profits.

Introduction
Currency exposure is unavoidable whenever assets, liabilities or cash flows are
denominated in a foreign currency. Yet the decision to hedge is not as simple as
choosing between being hedged and unhedged. What is the objective of the
hedge? How much risk should be removed, over what horizon, with what
instrument and at what cost? Every hedge involves a trade-off. It can reduce
uncertainty around portfolio returns, revenues or costs, but it also incurs direct

costs through forward points, option premia and transaction expenses, as well as
an opportunity cost if the currency subsequently moves in the hedger’s favour. This
chapter develops a framework for making those decisions. Rather than treating FX
hedging as a standalone transaction, it considers currency risk within the investor’s
total portfolio or the corporate’s full set of exposures. It examines passive and
dynamic hedge ratios, currency overlay programmes, cash-flow and balance-sheet

hedging, and the additional complications created by restricted emerging market
currencies. The central argument is that there is no universally optimal hedge ratio:
the appropriate strategy depends on the hedger’s objective, constraints,
underlying exposures and tolerance for cost and risk.
4.1 The problem of defining the hedging "objective"
So far we have looked at currency returns from the perspective of an active investor.
The objective here is clear: maximise returns. But what about market participants

where FX risk is an unwanted by-product of underlying economic exposure. While
in theory simple to understand, a hedging objective is far more complicated to
implement in practice. The first challenge is that a hedging objective is not well-
defined. An investor aims to maximise returns; a "hedger" aims to offset underlying

### Page 65

The Currency Book
Deutsche Bank AG Page 65
currency exposure, but to what end? The most frequent objective of hedging is to
minimise earnings volatility for a corporate or returns volatility for a passive investor
versus a benchmark. In practice, this objective faces multiple difficulties.
First, particularly for corporates, there is no widely accepted and well-defined
"benchmark" against which to measure the success or cost of a hedging decision.

Second, the objective of earnings volatility minimisation may not be achievable
because of imperfect foresight: cashflows and balance sheet exposure are not
infinitely predictable and hedgeable into the future. Third, hedging involves a cost.
There is the direct cost that needs to be paid to purchase the underlying hedging
instrument, usually in the form of forward points. There is also the indirect cost of
the "counter-factual" exchange-rate that would have prevailed had the underlying

exposure not been hedged. The simple point is that there is an opportunity cost to
hedging; and that the volatility minimisation or other objective needs to be set in the
context of this cost.

4.3 Currency hedging for corporates
While many of the principles of dynamic FX hedging also apply to corporates, the
underlying hedging problem is different. Institutional investors typically hedge
currency exposure embedded within assets whose value changes over time.

Because the asset value itself fluctuates, a 100% hedge does not necessarily
eliminate risk and may even increase volatility if currency and asset returns
naturally offset one another. The objective is therefore to manage FX risk as part of
the total portfolio.
Corporates, by contrast, are usually hedging known or forecast foreign-currency
cash flows, revenues, costs, assets or liabilities. Because the size of the underlying
exposure is often known in advance, a corporate can in principle hedge a much

larger proportion of its FX risk. The challenge is therefore less about portfolio
optimisation and more about balancing risk reduction against hedging costs and
opportunity costs. While investors ask how much currency risk should be retained
within a portfolio, corporates are typically focused on how much uncertainty
around future cash flows should be eliminated and at what cost.

assets or liabilities are denominated in a currency other than the entity's functional

EUR asset on its USD balance sheet. Once they start billing out of their EUR
subsidiary, for accounting purposes a regular balance sheet hedge may be
required as the parent company's functional currency is still USD. In
summary, this type of hedging hedges assets and liabilities on corporate
balance sheets that are not in the entity's functional currency. A typical

balance sheet hedging strategy may be rolling short dated forwards.
n Deal-contingent and M&A hedging: Continuing with the example, say the
US company instead chooses to merge or acquire a European company.
Thus, the cross-border merger and acquisition (M&A) now creates a need
for hedging the foreign currency. In certain respects, this type of hedge is
conceptually similar to cash flow hedges, as it is based on a potentially
unpredictable transaction, but with additional complexities, including

whether the underlying transaction is in stock or cash.
The corporate hedging decision
The “bottom-up” hedging decision involves a large number of parameters the
optimal mix of which is unique for each firm. While it is true that many firms share
the same basic exposure profile, each firm’s strategy can vary dramatically
depending on its objectives and constraints. Empirical investigations into the
hedging practices of multinational firms show that hedging decisions are

influenced by a large number of case-specific factors. These include accounting
treatment, competitive price concerns and recent hedging outcomes. Overall
empirical studies of corporate risk management suggest that a single, clear and
value-maximising policy for risk management is not obvious (Brown, GW 2001).
The micro hedging decision will also vary depending on market conditions. In all,
the evidence cited above argues against recommending a universally applicable

hedging programme for all firms.
The “top-down” elements of the hedging decision relate to the market drivers of FX
that we identified in the earlier sections of the report. Unlike the multiple parameters
involved in the micro hedging decision, the macro decision boils down to market
variables: where does the Treasurer or CFO expect the exchange rate(s) to be over
the relevant hedging period and how does this relate to the underlying exposure of
the firm? One approach is for the hedger to make a discretionary decision based on

his or her underlying assessment of market drivers. Another is to adopt a rules-
based approach based on the systematic excess returns we have identified in the
FX market. Such an approach would be no different to the dynamic hedging
strategy employed by a passive "profit-constrained" investor discussed in the
section above.

Dynamic FX hedging for corporates
Dynamic FX hedging differs for corporates and institutional investors. Corporates
typically hedge known exposures tied to contracted or forecast cash flows, rather
than currency risk embedded in assets whose value can grow over time. In
principle, they can remove much of this risk by hedging 100% of the identified
exposure. However, a full hedge can carry opportunity costs. For multi-currency

exposures, it may overlook diversification benefits when currency moves partly
offset one another. It can also lock in hedging costs before the full-period outcome
is known, potentially causing corporates, like institutional investors, to forgo gains
from favourable currency moves. For corporates, the hedging framework should
aim to reduce these opportunity costs without materially increasing risk.
For companies with a single-currency exposure, signal-based hedging frameworks

can be useful. Rather than relying solely on forward points, hedge decisions can
incorporate carry, momentum and valuation signals to assess whether hedging is
likely to be more or less beneficial. Grover (2024) suggests this can reduce the
opportunity cost of fully hedging, or of hedging based only on current forward
points.
For companies with multiple currency exposures, hedging becomes more
portfolio-like. Managing the joint risk of the currency basket through Value-at-Risk

or cross-currency correlation analysis can help capture diversification benefits that
a full-hedge approach may miss. Grover and Nandy (2025) show that, over recent
decades, a holistic FX framework has delivered lower-cost and higher-return
hedging outcomes than fully hedging each exposure. This result is particularly clear
for euro-based exporters, where carry costs have been higher and correlations
between euro crosses have been lower, increasing the value of a portfolio-based

approach. Overall, corporate hedging is best approached as a two-step process:

n Use currency signals such as carry, momentum and valuation to reduce the

This approach recognises that, while corporates and investors start from different

optimise FX exposure within a broader investment portfolio, whereas corporates
seek to minimise uncertainty around future cash flows while controlling the cost of
doing so.
4.4 Hedging emerging market currencies
While the framework above is applicable to both G10 and EM currencies, some EM
currencies require additional consideration when hedging. In particular, currencies

that are subject to current account restrictions, capital controls, or both operate
under different market constraints. We refer to these as restricted markets. Many
of these currencies trade offshore through non-deliverable forwards (NDFs), and
foreign institutional investors and corporations have historically relied on NDFs to
hedge their currency exposures.
However, offshore markets are not always the most cost-effective venue for
hedging. On average, onshore forward curves have traded around 40 basis points

below offshore curves over the past decade. That creates a potential opportunity for
investors. By accessing onshore markets, they may be able to achieve lower
hedging costs. For currencies, where operational challenges exist, tactical hedging
via cheaper onshore forward markets can further improve hedging efficiency
(Grover, 2026 and Harvey et al., 2025).
Another important consideration when hedging EM currencies is the cross-
currency basis, which represents a hidden component of hedging costs for

corporate and institutional investors (Harvey and Friedemann, 2023). Although it
has attracted significant attention since the 2008-09 financial crisis, its role in
emerging markets is often overlooked despite being larger and more volatile than
in many developed markets. The cross-currency basis can create either costs or
opportunities for hedgers and issuers. While instruments such as cross-currency
basis swaps can be used to manage these exposures, liquidity and market access

vary considerably across EM currencies, and usage is typically concentrated at
longer tenors. As most shorter-dated EM hedging remains centred on FX forwards
and swaps, investors should remain aware of the cross-currency basis when
assessing the true cost of hedging.

Introduction
The foreign exchange market has always evolved alongside the technologies,
institutions and regulations through which money moves across borders. Payment
systems determine which currencies are easiest to use, where liquidity
accumulates and who controls the infrastructure supporting cross-border
transactions. This final chapter considers how technology, regulation and
geopolitics may reshape FX market structure, payments and currency demand.

5.1 Technology and market structure
While electronification aimed to improve the FX market by reducing risk and
increasing efficiency, it has inadvertently introduced greater complexity and
fragmentation. This has resulted in a more decentralised and dispersed market
structure, increasing operational risks such as vulnerability to cyberattacks, even in
a traditionally liquid market like FX. Despite these challenges, technological
innovation continues to drive change. Distributed ledger technology (DLT), with its

decentralised and shared database, offers potential benefits like improved
efficiency, transparency, and reduced settlement risk. Furthermore, automation
promises cost reductions and efficiency gains in trading processes, while AI and
machine learning are being increasingly applied to price prediction, risk
management, and fraud detection. The interplay between these technological
advancements and the evolving market structure will continue to shape the future

of the FX market.
5.2 Stablecoins and payment rails as the new geopolitical battle lines
The dollar is hugely dominant in FX markets today. According to BIS, 88% of FX
transactions have a USD leg. The USD acts as a global vehicle currency. It offers the
deepest pools of liquidity which makes it cheaper to trade third currencies via their
USD crosses. The USD accounts for 50% of messages exchanged on SWIFT for
cross-border payment, and its share in trade invoicing far exceeds the US'

economic share of global trade. Cross-border USD payments today are mostly

But Sachdeva and Saravelos (2025) show that the dollar correspondent banking

Keeping the dollar dominant in payments and transactions will be of significant
importance to the US in a world of heightened geopolitical competition. Remaining

external surpluses are built. The world's willingness to save in USD in turn lowers
the cost of USD financing which is a big advantage for the US government and
corporates. To support the dollar's role in payments in the future, the US appears to

be turning to the power of distributed ledger technology and a private form of
money: stablecoins.
Stablecoins are new forms of private digital money tokens hosted on blockchains
that maintain a peg to a fiat currency. Under GENIUS Act regulation, USD
stablecoins will be fully backed by liquid, short-term USD reserves like T-bills to help
maintain their 1:1 peg with the USD. 99% of stablecoins outstanding today are
linked to the USD. They are issued and traded on distributed ledgers, making them

accessible anywhere through the internet. Their on-chain attributes mean
stablecoins offer the possibility of near-instant settlement - where all aspects of
monetary value transfer from messaging to reconciliation happen together in the
form of a ledger update. Stablecoins do however disintermediate the Fed from the
settlement process for international payments in USD. Europe and China  have
taken a very different approach thus far to digital money, concentrating more efforts

on central bank digital currencies as their response to the future of cross-border
payments.
5.3 Tokenization and programmability
Stablecoins are arguably just a subset of a broader trend towards tokenisation that
could open many new possibilities for how FX markets operate in the future.
Tokenisation is the process of creating claims on underlying money or assets, that
can then be represented as digital tokens and made transferrable on a shared
infrastructure. Stablecoins are a new type of tokenized private money, backed by

cash, bank deposits or bills. But tokenisation also extends into existing commercial
bank deposits, central bank money, and traditional assets like equities, money
markets and bonds. In the earlier section we covered how stablecoins might help
with cross-border payments, by offering 24/7 infrastructure to move USD that does
not rely on traditional banking relationships and hours. But tokenized money can
also help with other aspects of the FX settlement process - the exchange of

currencies for each other, or for assets. Because FX trading takes place primarily in
the over-the-counter market, once a trade is executed and enters the post-trade

Programmability allows certain automated rules and conditions to be embedded
into money processes. This is often implemented through smart contracts that
execute transactions when predefined conditions are met, helping improve timing

frictions, settlement risk, or the need to pre fund and trap liquidity to meet payment
obligations. In a programmable world, a payment can be released only if a
corresponding asset is transferred, or an FX transaction can settle only if both
currency legs are ready for delivery. This technology can thus support more
synchronised settlement processes, linking cash, securities and collateral
movements through predefined rules and automated execution. While tokenised

money can improve payments and settlement on a standalone basis, its full
potential may emerge when combined with increasingly tokenised securities.

5.4 Concluding thoughts
The foreign exchange market is one of the most innovative and rapidly evolving
financial markets in the world. Over the past two decades, advances in technology,

electronic trading, data and market infrastructure have transformed how
currencies are traded, priced and managed, placing FX at the forefront of broader
changes in global finance. Yet because currencies sit at the intersection of national
economies, the FX market remains fundamentally connected to international trade
and cross-border investment. It is therefore increasingly exposed to the forces
reshaping the global economic landscape—from geopolitical fragmentation and

changing trade relationships to shifts in regulation, payments infrastructure and
