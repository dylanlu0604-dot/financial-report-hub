银⾏Agent的落地⽅式，正在从单点试验转向更系统的⼯程化建设。
9⽉初，浙商银⾏⼀笔Agent基础软硬件项⽬披露中标结果，接近6500万元的报价，成为年内公

设平台和运⾏观测等环节。
银⾏正在为Agent花更多的钱，⽽这些钱正在投向更底层的⼯程能⼒。
⼏乎同期，服贸会、外滩⼤会上，银⾏⾼管开始更多讨论如何批量开发Agent、上线后怎么评
估、怎样持续管理；
部分⾦融科技公司甚⾄将Agent平台定义为“超级⼯⼚”，欲把开发、部署、评测和管理纳⼊同
⼀套体系。
资⾦流向和⾏业讨论指向了同⼀个变化：银⾏的⽬标正在从落地零散的Agent，转向⼀套能够反

复⽣产、调⽤和管理Agent的基础能⼒。
资⾦流向底座
真实业务中，Agent需要调⽤知识、⼯具、业务系统执⾏任务；
这套规则落地银⾏业时的特殊性在于，银⾏不仅要检查输出结果，也要控制任务的执⾏过程。
⼀家股份⾏数据架构师向枢纽表⽰，不是所有场景都适合直接交给Agent；
例如，意图识别、合同要素抽取等“⾮标准输⼊、标准输出”的任务容易落地，但信贷审批、
理财规划等复杂流程，需要把模型嵌⼊既有⼯作流，由规则、权限和⼈⼯节点共同控制。
这也改变了银⾏科技项⽬固有的研发与交付⽅式。
传统科技项⽬通常由业务提出需求、科技负责开发；
Agent进⼊⽣产后，业务规则、SOP和经验判断需要被转化为模型能够识别和执⾏的逻辑，业务
⼈员需要深地参与测试和调整。
上述数据架构师表⽰，在Agent的⾦融场景开发模式中，业务专家需要深度参与知识和规则的沉

延伸到共同开发和持续调整。
⽽当Agent从试点⾛向批量，原本由各个项⽬分别完成的开发、评测和运⾏管理，也随之出现标
准化需求。
近期的采购已经出现对应变化。
枢纽统计发现，今年上半年，⾦融⾏业⼤模型中标项⽬达到406个，同⽐增⻓110%，其中银⾏机
构在项⽬数量和⾦额上的占⽐均接近半数；
⾃去年下半年开始，Agent类应⽤项⽬明显增加，银⾏AI建设逐渐由模型部署和算⼒扩容，⾛向
更具体的应⽤层。
这其中，浙商银⾏接近6500万元的Agent基础软硬件项⽬，已经覆盖算⼒、知识⼯程、模型和
Agent运⾏环节，建设范围延伸⾄⽀撑后续Agent开发和运⾏的基础环境；
同时，浦发银⾏采购Agent创设平台，重点落在Agent的批量创建和管理；
恒丰银⾏则单独建设Agent观测能⼒，把投⼊延伸到上线后的运⾏监测。
上述⼏类项⽬，分别落在Agent⽣产体系的不同环节，说明银⾏的投⼊对象正在由具体应⽤，继
续向开发和运⾏基础设施延伸。
相似的变化，也反映在了科技部⻔职责和招聘需求上。
⼀位⾦融科技公司⼈⼠向枢纽反映，随着Agent数量增加，银⾏科技部⻔需要提供统⼀的开发和

运⾏环境，并处理资源调⽤、Agent协同和治理等问题。
枢纽注意到，近期宁波银⾏相关岗位出现Agent⼯作流构建和技术框架研发；
平安银⾏则招聘AI Agent评测⼯程师，职责涉及⼯具调⽤、多步推理以及安全和性能评测。
从岗位设置看，Agent进⼊批量建设后，银⾏对⼯程能⼒的需求已经延伸到⼯作流、⼯具调⽤、
评测和运⾏管理等环节。
财富、信贷、客服仍按照各⾃的知识、流程和⻛险边界开发，但其开发、验证和运⾏管理，正
逐渐形成⼀套相对统⼀的⽅法；
当Agent进⼊规模化建设，这套⽣产⽅法本⾝，也将成为银⾏AI投⼊的新底座。
流程重新接线
Agent进⼊银⾏核⼼业务后，真正复杂的，是原有流程⾥的⼯作怎样重新拆分。
信贷是⼀个典型场景。
今年以来，苏州银⾏相继推进⽂档识别、尽调、AI审批、放款审核和贷后管理等项⽬；
上海银⾏也在对公授信的营销、尽调和贷后环节布局Agent。

这些项⽬分布在⼀笔授信的不同阶段：
材料读取、数据提取、财务分析等任务相对标准，更容易交给机器，越往⻛险判断和审批环节
⾛，对业务经验的依赖越⾼；
⼀组财务指标是否异常、哪些问题需要继续追问、同样的指标放在不同产业周期中意味着什
么，往往没有固定答案，很多判断沉淀在资深信贷⼈员⻓期积累的经验⾥。
⽬前，亦有⾦融科技公司在尝试把这类经验判断进⼀步拆解、沉淀，并重新嵌⼊信贷流程。
神州信息AI创新中⼼总经理晋梅介绍，其公司推出CreditMind⽅案会把历史尽调报告、审批意⻅
以及贷款后续表现重新组织，从中提取资深信贷⼈员判断⻛险时使⽤的逻辑和经验，再⽤于新
的审批任务。
在新的授信流程中，系统先处理客⼾材料和基础分析，再调⽤历史案例和专家经验补充⻛险提
⽰，最终交由审批⼈员判断；
这类尝试的价值，在于把过去主要沉淀在个⼈经验中的部分判断，转化成可以被Agent调⽤的
Skill，再重新放回审批流程。
不过，能够被沉淀的主要是相对明确的判断逻辑，真正依赖具体客⼾、⾏业环境和⻓期经验形
成的判断，仍然需要⼈完成。

在这套组织架构之下，Agent进⼊信贷流程，将把原有⼯作拆成更细的任务：标准化信息处理给
机器、可提炼经验转成Skill、关键⻛险判断留给⼈。
可以观察到的是，这种拆分已向更⼤范围扩展。
浦发银⾏披露，今年上半年已有超过440个AI应⽤，其中220个Agent实现⼯程化落地，同时上线
108条AI流⽔线，数字劳动⼒承担的⼯作量超过2500⼈年。
“AI流⽔线”⽐单个Agent数量更能反映流程层⾯的变化：不同AI能⼒按照业务顺序被重新组
织，机器连续承担其中⼀部分任务，再在需要判断、授权和承担责任的节点交回⼈⼯。
浦发银⾏⾸席⼈⼯智能科学家郑波将⾦融AI进⼊⽣产概括为三个标准：进⼊核⼼业务流程，承
担过去由⼈完成的关键任务，并改善客⼾体验、经营效率或⻛险管理。
对银⾏⽽⾔，Agent深⼊业务的程度，越来越取决于流程能被拆得多细。
哪些⼯作可以稳定交给机器，哪些经验能够沉淀成Skill，哪些判断必须留给⼈，这些边界越清
楚，Agent越有可能真正嵌⼊核⼼业务。
进⼊运营之后
Agent进⼊业务流程后，银⾏管理的对象，不只是⼀套系统能否正常运⾏，还包括它的业务效果

能否持续满⾜要求、是否值得继续投⼊，以及出现异常后流程能不能被顺利接住。
⾸先要解决的是持续校正。

市场有⻛险，投资需谨慎。本⽂不构成个⼈投资建议，也未考虑到个别⽤⼾特殊的投资⽬标、财务状况或需要。⽤⼾应考虑本⽂
中的任何意⻅、观点或结论是否符合其特定状况。据此投资，责任⾃负。
Agent上线时通过测试，并不代表后续效果会保持不变；
业务规则、知识内容、客⼾表达都在变化，实际使⽤中还会不断出现新的反⾯案例，银⾏需要
继续判断，问题究竟来⾃知识缺失、流程设计，还是模型能⼒，再针对性调整。
来⾃⾦融科技公司的王军（化名）向枢纽表⽰，传统软件上线后更多围绕“运维”展开，Agent
则需要持续“运营”；
这种运营关注的不只是系统是否在线，还包括输出是否符合业务要求、问题出现在哪⾥，以及
后续该怎么修正。
这也让Agent上线后的评价变得更重要。
郑波谈及⾦融AI未来⼏年的竞争时，将⽣产能⼒、价值运营能⼒和组织进化列为关键；
浙商银⾏董事⻓陈海强则表⽰，科技投⼊要成为降本增效的收益项，通过使⽤频率、承担的⼯
作量、处理效率，以及客⼾转化和⻛险管理效果等数据，判断科技实际产出。

这些结果会进⼀步影响资源配置：能够稳定承担⼯作、形成业务价值的Agent可以继续迭代，⻓
期低频、效果有限的应⽤，则需要调整甚⾄退出。
对银⾏⽽⾔，另⼀项运营要求是业务连续性。
以授信为例，若Agent已经参与材料读取、尽调分析和审批流转，发⽣异常时，银⾏需要明确任
务停在哪⼀步、哪些结果已经进⼊后续流程、是否触发过审批动作，以及接下来由谁继续处
理；
涉及交易和系统操作时，还要确认指令是否已经执⾏，避免⼈⼯接管后重复操作，或者因状态
不清引发新的操作⻛险。
王军表⽰，⽬前业内已经开始讨论给Agent设置kill switch，但更难的是Agent停下来以后，业务
怎么继续。
枢纽注意到，如今这类需求，也已经进⼊银⾏的建设范围。
恒丰银⾏单独建设Agent观测能⼒，反映出银⾏对Agent的关注已经延伸到上线后的运⾏状态和异
常监测。
只有清楚Agent正在处理什么、执⾏到了哪⾥，异常发⽣后才有条件及时接管；
持续校正决定⼀个Agent能不能越⽤越准，价值评价决定它值不值得继续留下，异常接管则决定

银⾏敢不敢把更多⼯作交给它。
当Agent真正成为业务流程中的⻓期节点，运营能⼒也就成为规模化落地之后新的分⽔岭。

INDIA: WHY IS A MIDDLE CLASS STRUGGLING TO EMERGE DESPITE RECORD GROWTH?
1 We use the methodology of López-Calva and Ortiz-Juárez (2014), who define the middle class as households with a less than 4% probability of falling back into
poverty within four years, and whose daily income ranges from $17 to $98 when adjusted for 2021 purchasing power parity (PPP). Due to the lack of reliable income

surveys in India, these thresholds are applied to the consumer expenditure data provided by the World Bank. This approach to measuring real purchasing power tends
to slightly underestimate the middle class in terms of income, as savings are not taken into account; however, it remains the metric used by the World Bank.
Due to robust economic growth, India’s contribution to global GDP has increased by 1.8 times over the past twenty years,
reaching 8.2% in 2025. However, this achievement conceals a significant issue: India is failing to develop a substantial,

consumer-driven middle class. The underlying cause is the predominance of employment in low-value-added and largely
informal sectors. The few high-value-added manufacturing sectors with strong job potential such as electrical equipment,
electronics, automotive, chemicals and pharmaceuticals, are hindered by an unfavourable business environment, while a
skills shortage is obstructing their advancement up the value chain. Furthermore, the highest-paying service jobs, particu-

larly in IT, are threatened by artificial intelligence.
INDIA STRUGGLES TO DEVELOP A MIDDLE CLASS
India has one of the highest growth rates among the world’s major eco-
nomies (+7.3% annually over the past decade, excluding the pandemic),
yet its consumer middle class remains surprisingly small. In current
dollars, total consumption in India appears very high (USD 2,241 bn
in 2025), but this figure is largely a reflection of the country’s vast
population. By way of comparison, it is 3.5 times lower than that of

China, and per capita consumption at purchasing power parity (PPP)
is 1.3 times lower than that of Indonesia, the Philippines and Vietnam.
According to our estimates 1, the middle class accounts for only 1.6%
of India’s population, equating to just 24 million people. This figure is
lower than that of Thailand and Vietnam, each with over 30 million,
and significantly less than China’s middle class, which is estimated
at 490 million people, or 35% of the population, using the same me -

thodology. Furthermore, the disparity between urban and rural areas
remains particularly pronounced, as urban households consume, on
average, 1.6 times more than their rural counterparts. More than 60%
of the Indian population consumes less than the national average.
Only the top urban deciles, which represent only 5% of the total popu-
lation, have genuine discretionary spending power. Although consump-
tion patterns have evolved over the last decade, evidenced by a decline

in the share of food spending (-4.3 percentage points, now at 34.8%
of total spending) in favour of transportation and communications
(+3.8 pp, now at 12.4%), purchases of durable goods, which account for
only 7% of urban household spending, remain marginal. Furthermore,
these purchases are increasingly financed by consumer credit rather
than by rising incomes.
THE LABOUR MARKET SUFFERS FROM STRUCTURAL WEAKNESSES
This weakness in the middle class and in household consumption can

be explained, in particular, by the structural weaknesses of the labour
market. The employment rate, which stood at 52.7% in 2025 according
to the International Labor Organization (ILO), is significantly lower than
that of its Asian neighbours: 65.6% in Indonesia and 71.3% in Vietnam.
Over the past ten years, employment has experienced only modest
growth (an average of +2.4% per year), while GDP growth has averaged
nearly 6% per year (including the pandemic). The services sector, which

accounts for 33.2% of employment, has been the primary driver of job
creation (+3.2% per year), significantly outpacing the manufacturing
sector (+1.4%) and agriculture (+0.1%), despite agriculture being the
country’s largest employer, accounting for 41.5% of the labour force.
This trend is insufficient to absorb the 10 to 12 million new entrants to
the labour market each year.
Furthermore, the traditional pathway for labour transition from agri -

culture to industry has become severely restricted. Over the past de -
cade, employment in manufacturing has barely kept pace with growth:
the employment elasticity with respect to growth reached only 0.1 for
this sector. However, the contrast between the formal and informal
sectors is striking: the employment elasticity of the formal manufactu-
ring sector reached 0.7 over the same period. Consequently, the decline
in informal employment, which accounts for 73.4% of total manufactu-

ring employment (but only 38% of the sector’s value added), has offset
this positive trend. Furthermore, in labour-intensive sectors such as
textiles, apparel and wood, the increase in value added stems primarily
from productivity gains rather than job creation, which significantly
limits the availability of low-skilled jobs in the country.
Several structural challenges hinder job creation in the manufacturing
sector: an unfavourable business environment (particularly regarding

access to land), the high cost of capital for SMEs, a lack of export
competitiveness in labour-intensive sectors, which are penalised by
less advantageous tariff conditions compared to the rest of Asia, and,
above all, labour market rigidity, which obstructs the size of companies
and, consequently, job creation (65% of companies have fewer than
50 employees).
INSUFFICIENT JOBS IN BOTH QUANTITY AND QUALITY
Not only is the number of jobs created insufficient, but their quality is

also subpar, which adversely affects income growth and the develop -
ment of a substantial middle class. According to the ILO, 87% of total
employment is informal and concentrated in low-value-added, low-
skilled roles. Approximately 20% of the population earns 79% of labour
income. This concentration reflects the low skill level of existing jobs:
88.2% of Indian workers have jobs that require minimal or no skills.
The issue is not a shortage of graduates but rather a mismatch between

their education and the actual needs of businesses. This results in a
gradual decline mobility among workers and a higher unemployment
rate among graduates compared to those without a degree (13.5% ver-
sus 1.3% in 2025, according to the ILO).
The lack of skills that align with the demands of the labour market
obstructs employment opportunities in the few high-potential manu -
facturing sectors. According to our econometric estimates, electronics

and IT, electrical engineering, automotive, chemicals, and, to a les -
ser extent, pharmaceuticals exhibit strong growth, high employment
elasticity, and high productivity (see chart). They therefore represent
potential sources of high-quality jobs. However, these sectors remain
largely underdeveloped: in 2025, they accounted for only 1.4% of jobs
and 5.9% of India’s GDP .

AI may also impede the creation of the highest-paying jobs, which are
essential for expanding the middle-class consumer base.
Agriculture, construction, trade and transportation remain largely im -
mune to direct job displacement due to AI. Therefore, the macroecono-

mic impact of the immediate shock is expected to be minimal. However,
these sectors are characterised by the lowest wage levels. Conversely,
IT, finance and insurance account for less than 3% of total employment
but employ the most highly skilled workers. Ironically, these are the
very segments most threatened by automation.
While sector-specific data does not currently indicate a widespread de-
cline, there is a notable deceleration in job creation for routine tasks, as

confirmed by a study conducted by the ICRIER (Indian Council for Re -
search on International Economic Relations). Although new job roles are
emerging, their number is expected to remain limited, particularly as
the skills required surpass the capabilities of the existing graduate pool.
REFORMS ARE HEADING IN THE RIGHT DIRECTION BUT ARE INSUFFICIENT
Since 2019, the Modi government has rolled out a series of measures
aimed at developing industry, attracting foreign direct investment, and

integrating into global trade after decades of protectionist policies.
Corporate subsidy programs, such as Production-Linked Incentives,
have bolstered the growth of the most promising sectors identified
earlier. Similarly, the implementation of the labour reform, which came
into force in late 2025, could facilitate business expansion and the
creation of formal jobs. However, its actual impact will depend on its
adoption by the states, which possess significant autonomy over labour

law, and on its effective implementation at local level. The free trade
agreement signed between India and the European Union in late Janua-
ry 2026 and anticipated to take effect in 2027 at the earliest, aims to
mitigate the tariff disadvantages faced by labour-intensive industries.
The EU has pledged to abolish tariffs on Indian textiles and apparel,
which currently range from 4% to 12%.
However, these measures alone will not suffice to create high-quality

jobs unless the education system aligns with the needs of Indian bu -
sinesses. It is therefore imperative that India continue to improve its
business environment and reform its education system to promote job
creation in high-paying, high-value-added sectors. Without concrete
measures, the “demographic window”, which is expected to remain fa-
vourable for another 15 to 25 years, could close before India succeeds in
establishing a solid industrial foundation and a substantial middle class.

Melbourne Institute Leading Index, which indicates the likely

The mix does raise some questions about the extent to which

Tokenisation has the potential to transform the way financial markets operate, from the 
movement of money and assets to the management of liquidity and risk. In this paper, we 
focus on tokenised forms of money and their impact on FX markets. 
Many emerging use cases for tokenised money are driven by a desire to reduce frictions 
associated with cross-border payments and FX settlement. Stablecoins enable 24/7 
cross-border transfers but remain overwhelmingly US dollar-denominated. As a result, 
they are currently more relevant as digital dollar infrastructure and for bridge-currency 
arrangements, often creating a "stablecoin sandwich" in which the US dollar serves as an 
intermediary between two currency legs, rather than providing a complete solution to the 
two-currency FX settlement problem 
Tokenisation may play a bigger role in transforming the combined securities and FX 
settlement cycles involved in cross-border capital flows. This is an area of increasing 
complexity, as delays or failures in one part of the transaction chain can affect the timely 
completion of the wider transaction, increasing operational, liquidity, and settlement 
risks. For example, following the transition to T+1 securities settlement in North America 
and with Europe preparing to move to T+1, the need to coordinate FX, cash, and securities 
settlement across institutions, jurisdictions, and infrastructures is already increasing. 
Tokenisation enables programmability, which allows atomic settlement and a range of 
operational and control functions across financial market infrastructures, including real-
time liquidity management, margin management, collateral mobilisation and substitution, 
and automated compliance checks. Current implementations have focused primarily on 
supporting corporate treasury and liquidity management activities involving FX. However, 
the settlement of tokenised securities and the associated FX transactions may offer even 
greater efficiencies by enabling synchronised settlement and reducing some of the 
operational, funding, and coordination frictions associated with shorter settlement cycles. 
Interoperability between different currencies, tokenised money arrangements, and 
payment systems remains one of the key challenges facing the development of tokenised 
cross-border payments and FX settlement. Several initiatives are exploring solutions 
involving tokenised central bank reserves or hybrid models that combine tokenised 
commercial bank money with central bank money. These may be particularly relevant for 
multi-currency settlement frameworks, preserving the two-tier monetary system while 
supporting settlement finality and more integrated cross-border processes. In parallel,

Introduction
Tokenised money refers to digital representations of money that are recorded and
transferable on shared or distributed ledger infrastructure. This includes both state
and privately issued fiat-denominated forms of tokenised money, including
stablecoins, tokenised commercial bank deposits and tokenised central bank
money. 1 The potential role of tokenised money is increasingly being explored by

both corporates and institutional investors. Several use cases have emerged
ranging from cross-border payments, wholesale settlement, treasury and liquidity
management, trade finance and capital market infrastructure. More recently,
attention has expanded beyond using tokenised money for cross-border payments
towards models that can support the coordination and settlement of FX
transactions.
While tokenised money can support more efficient payments, FX settlement often

requires the conditional coordination of payments across currencies. Unlike a
simple payment, FX settlement requires the exchange of two currencies to occur
simultaneously, creating a need for a synchronisation or programmability layer
that enables Payment-versus-Payment (PvP) settlement and reduces settlement
risk. Tokenised money operating on programmable ledger-based infrastructure
can support this by embedding rules and conditions directly into settlement

processes, facilitating the settlement of both tokenised money and other tokenised
assets.
The settlement process, particularly  for wholesale FX transactions involving
financial institutions and large corporations, is exposed to settlement risk, often
referred to as Herstatt risk. This arises when one party delivers its currency but the
counterparty fails to fulfil its obligation. Cross-border transactions are particularly
vulnerable because time zone differences can create a window in which one side

has settled while the other has not. Over recent decades, this risk has been
materially reduced in major currencies through infrastructure such as CLS, helping
to support a large, liquid and operationally resilient FX market.
CLS mitigates FX settlement risk through PvP settlement, ensuring that one
currency is transferred only if the corresponding currency is delivered, while
reducing funding requirements via multilateral netting and liquidity optimisation

tools, generating liquidity savings of over 96% for settlement members. This
liquidity efficiency is important in wholesale markets because it determines the
amount of liquidity required for the system to settle its full volume of transactions
safely.
However, these benefits are not universal. Many emerging-market currencies
subject to current- or capital-account restrictions remain outside the CLS
ecosystem and continue to rely on correspondent banking networks or local

clearing systems. Settlement in these markets often involves bilateral pre-funding,
fragmented payment chains and complex liquidity management, creating
operational and funding frictions that can increase the cost and complexity of
cross-border transactions.

counterparty (CCP), payment instructions are exchanged, and settlement

FX settlement frictions: corporations versus institutional investors
The challenges associated with FX settlement are often most acute when
transactions are cross-border, requiring the movement of currencies across

different jurisdictions, payment systems, market infrastructures and operating
hours. These challenges can arise whether FX settlement supports an underlying
commercial payment or forms part of a broader securities transaction.
Corporations and FX
For corporations, FX spot settlement is typically a standalone transaction that
supports cross-border trade, commercial payments and treasury activities. Cross-
border payments and liquidity management are closely linked to the efficient

functioning of the FX market. Corporates frequently need to convert currencies to
support international trade, meet cross-border payment obligations and fund
activities across multiple jurisdictions. More broadly, treasury functions rely on the
seamless movement of currencies to manage cash positions, fund subsidiaries and
address short-term liquidity needs.
Effective liquidity management facilitates domestic and cross-border payment
obligations, supports the optimisation of cash flows and enables corporates to

respond to changing funding requirements. These activities often involve access to
wholesale FX markets to convert currencies and mobilise liquidity across
jurisdictions. During periods of market volatility, the ability to execute FX
transactions and move liquidity efficiently becomes particularly important,
allowing institutions to respond swiftly to changing market conditions and
maintain confidence in their operations.

frictions associated with cross-border payments may exceed those arising from the
FX conversion itself.
Institutional investors and FX
For institutional investors buying or selling securities across jurisdictions, FX spot
settlement is part of a broader cross-border securities transaction, where FX
settlement, cash movements and securities settlement must be coordinated across

multiple intermediaries and jurisdictions. When an investor purchases a foreign
equity or bond, they must first obtain the currency required to complete the
transaction. As a result, cross-border investments involve two interconnected
settlement processes: FX settlement, where one currency is exchanged for another
(PvP), and securities settlement, where cash is exchanged for the asset through a
Delivery-versus-Payment (DvP) mechanism. Where currencies are not eligible for

settlement through PvP infrastructures such as CLS, investors may rely on
correspondent banking networks to settle the FX transaction. In these cases, the
two currency legs are typically settled through separate payment systems, leaving
participants exposed to residual settlement risk.
International investors do not typically interact directly with domestic market
infrastructures such as DTCC in the United States. Instead, they access these
markets through custodians, which act as intermediaries between investors and

local settlement systems. Custodians are responsible for safekeeping assets,
managing settlement instructions, coordinating cash movements and arranging
funding across jurisdictions. When a foreign security is purchased, the custodian
must ensure that the appropriate settlement currency is available before the
transaction can be completed through the relevant central securities depository.
This creates a dependency between the FX funding trade and the securities

settlement process, meaning that delays or frictions in obtaining the required
currency can have direct implications for the timely settlement of the underlying
security. The ongoing transition from T+2 to T+1 security settlement cycles, and the

Key challenges for institutional investors under compressed timelines
The move to shorter securities settlement cycles has increased the operational
challenge of coordinating processes. For example, under T+1, a UK investor

purchasing a US equity at the market close (9pm London time) may have only
around two hours before the CLS submission cut-off at 11pm to determine the final
USD funding requirement, execute the associated GBP/USD transaction and
submit it to CLS for settlement. Once the FX transaction settles and USD funds are
made available to the custodian, the securities transaction can proceed through the
relevant securities clearing and settlement infrastructure. Missing this window

may require investors to pre-fund transactions, creating cash drag and unwanted
currency exposure, or settle the FX transaction outside CLS, potentially increasing
settlement risk and transaction costs.
Cross-border transactions involve a range of operational and settlement
challenges, particularly under compressed timelines. Local currency cut-off times
constrain the window available to execute and settle FX transactions, while
settlement risk remains where trades cannot be processed through CLS and must

settle bilaterally. Currency controls and market restrictions may further complicate
settlement in certain emerging-market currencies and necessitate pre-funding.
Shorter settlement cycles also leave less time for trade matching, confirmation,
settlement instruction processing and the resolution of failures, increasing the
importance of coordinating multiple post-trade processes.2
As FX settlement, cash movements and securities settlement occur across

separate infrastructures and timelines, shorter settlement cycles increase the
importance of post-trade coordination. Although the  Global Foreign Exchange
Committee (GFXC) described North America's transition to T+1 as operationally

trade coordination. A further move to T+0 would amplify these challenges ,
sharpening the trade-off between risk reduction and operational efficiency.

Tokenised money on programmable infrastructure
Tokenised money on programmable infrastructure offers a potential approach to
addressing some of the challenges outlined in the previous section. Tokenised
money refers to digital representations of money that are recorded and transferable
on shared or distributed ledger infrastructure. In many current implementations, its
use is enabled by two key elements: distributed ledger technology (DLT) and

programmability. DLT provides a shared infrastructure on which multiple
institutions can record and observe ownership changes in a common ledger,
potentially reducing the need for reconciliation across separate systems.
Programmability refers to the ability to embed executable logic, such as
conditions, rules and constraints, directly into instruments, or via smart
contracts. Such functionality can range from basic automation to more
sophisticated smart contract applications.

At the simplest level, programmability can automate settlement processes by
ensuring that transactions are executed only when predefined conditions are met,
such as Delivery-versus-Payment (DvP) in securities transactions and Payment-
versus-Payment (PvP) in FX settlement. More advanced applications can support
operational and control functions across financial market infrastructure, including
real-time liquidity management, margin management, collateral mobilisation and

substitution, and automated compliance checks. At a more sophisticated level,
programmability can coordinate multiple parties and obligations within a single
workflow, enabling applications such as trade finance arrangements, treasury and
cash management, automated disbursements and other multi-party payment
processes. These capabilities extend beyond process automation by embedding
business, risk and compliance logic directly into financial transactions (CCAF and

Fii, 2026). An emerging but largely underdeveloped area is the integration of
tokenised money with artificial intelligence and autonomous systems, where
programmable money interfaces could enable AI agents to initiate, manage and
execute financial transactions within predefined controls.
The approach to settlement risk mitigation in a tokenised environment differs from
the current CLS model, which achieves Payment-versus-Payment (PvP) settlement
through a centrally coordinated process operating within defined funding and

settlement windows. While CLS ensures that one currency is exchanged if and only
if the corresponding currency is delivered through a centralised settlement
mechanism, tokenised infrastructures could, in principle, embed PvP conditions
directly into the settlement process through programmable rules or smart
contracts on a shared ledger, enabling settlement to occur automatically when
predefined conditions are met. However, tokenised settlement models need not

rely solely on transaction-by-transaction atomic settlement. Some emerging
platforms, such as ClearToken, also incorporate queues, netting arrangements and
liquidity-saving mechanisms.

Stablecoin liquidity remains fragmented across issuers, networks and

comprehensive solution to PvP settlement across non-CLS currencies.

Tokenised commercial bank money
A tokenised deposit is not a new form of money, but a commercial bank deposit

represented or issued in tokenised form on a shared or tokenised ledger.
Economically, it remains a claim on a commercial bank, just as a conventional bank
deposit does today. The key difference is that the deposit, or rights linked to that
deposit, can be transferred and settled using tokenised infrastructure, smart
contracts and shared ledgers.

Current implementation models and infrastructure
Current models are developing along two main lines. The first is single-bank or
single-bank-group infrastructure, where blockchain-based deposit accounts or
tokenised commercial bank money are used between customers of the same
institution or banking group. J.P. Morgan’s Kinexys Digital Payments is an example
of an account-based blockchain deposit model rather than a deposit-token model.

It operates on a single-bank ledger, enabling participating J.P. Morgan clients to
transfer deposit balances across J.P. Morgan’s blockchain-based payments rail.
The BMW transaction illustrates this model: BMW executed a programmable on-
chain EUR/USD FX transaction using Kinexys Digital Payments , involving
automated balance checks, conditional auto-deposits, near-real-time FX execution
and transfers between BMW’s Blockchain Deposit Accounts in Frankfurt and New

York.
The second, more complex model is the interbank model, where tokenised bank
liabilities issued by different banks, in different currencies and potentially on
different ledgers, need to be exchanged or coordinated. Project Guardian explores
several approaches to these interoperability challenges. One model uses a liquidity
provider to facilitate the exchange of tokenised deposits in different currencies,
with off-chain FX prices fed into the shared ledger through an FX quote oracle.

third model, involving HSBC and OSTTRA, focuses on settlement interoperability,
using a shared-ledger PvP orchestration layer to match, confirm, fund and settle FX
cash flows, helping to reduce Herstatt risk and improve visibility across the
settlement.

Market adoption and FX settlement relevance
Commercial bank deposit claims, including tokenised deposits, have progressed
beyond early pilot stages and are seeing notable institutional adoption. While data

processed more than US$4 trillion since inception and averages more than US$7
billion daily in tokenised deposit movements for institutional payments. Other
initiatives include DBS Token Services, Citi Token Services, the USDF Consortium,
TassatPay, and projects from UOB and Standard Chartered.
The scale opportunity is potentially significant. Tokenised bank deposits and similar
bank-token models could support around  US$100–140 trillion  in annual

transaction flows by 2030, potentially rivalling or surpassing stablecoins in
institutional transaction volumes. This reflects the view that corporates and
financial institutions may prefer programmable forms of regulated bank money for
treasury, liquidity and FX settlement workflows where integration with existing
banking infrastructure, compliance processes and balance-sheet treatment is
important.
Overall, tokenised deposits appear more directly relevant to institutional FX than

stablecoins because they preserve the existing bank-money model while adding
programmability, potentially allowing new settlement capabilities to be integrated
more easily into existing banking, liquidity and risk-management frameworks.
Current implementation models appear more developed for transaction banking,
treasury and corporate payment use cases than for the broader wholesale FX
market, where achieving interoperability across banks, currencies and settlement

networks remains a more complex challenge.

models are likely to coexist with traditional RTGS systems, correspondent banking

liquidity may still need to be positioned across both traditional and tokenised
systems.

Tokenised central bank money
Rather than tokenising private bank money or relying on privately issued settlement
assets, an alternative approach is to make wholesale central bank money available
on DLT-based infrastructure. In this context, wholesale central bank money used in
DLT experiments is often referred to as wholesale central bank digital currency
(wCBDC) or, more precisely, tokenised central bank reserves. The latter term is

often preferred because it emphasises that the underlying asset, central bank
reserves, already exists in digital form, with the innovation lying primarily in the
technology used to represent and transfer it. While wCBDC is frequently used
interchangeably with tokenised central bank reserves, its definition varies across
institutions and may encompass a broader range of innovations in wholesale
central bank money that are not necessarily tokenisation-based (IMF, 2025).

Central bank reserves are already held digitally by eligible financial institutions
through RTGS systems such as T2 in Europe, Fedwire in the United States and
CHAPS in the United Kingdom. From this perspective, tokenised central bank
money initiatives represent not the introduction of a new form of public money, but
the extension of existing central bank liabilities into tokenised and programmable
environments.3
Although existing infrastructures already eliminate principal risk through DvP and

PvP mechanisms, tokenised reserves may support a more integrated settlement
process by bringing cash and assets onto the same ledger. This allows transactions
to be completed simultaneously and irrevocably using DLT and smart contracts,
while enabling programmability.
A number of initiatives have examined the use of multilateral platforms that make
wholesale central bank money available in tokenised form for cross-border
settlement. Examples include Project mBridge, Project Jura and Project Agorá.

Leveraging shared infrastructure and governance frameworks, these projects have
demonstrated real-time and atomic PvP settlement between commercial banks in
different jurisdictions using tokenised reserves. Smart contracts can further
automate and coordinate multicurrency transactions, improving operational
efficiency.

implement. Unlike stablecoins or tokenised deposits, they require extensive

coordination across central banks, legal jurisdictions and financial market
infrastructures. Cross-border deployment raises complex questions around
governance, access, interoperability, regulatory alignment, and the legal
recognition of settlement finality. As a result, industry attention has increasingly
broadened beyond pure wCBDC architectures towards hybrid models that
combine tokenised central bank money with tokenised commercial bank money.

networks remains uncertain.

Technology alone is not enough: risk reduction or
transformation?
Tokenised money is often discussed in terms of faster settlement, reduced

operational complexity and atomic execution. While these technologies may help
reduce certain forms of settlement risk, particularly where payment-versus-
payment (PvP) and delivery-versus-payment (DvP) mechanisms can be embedded
into the settlement process, they do not eliminate the need for liquidity provision,
risk management, netting arrangements or regulatory oversight. In many cases,
the effect may be better described as risk transformation rather than risk

elimination. Settlement risk may be reduced, but liquidity, operational, legal and
governance considerations remain.
Regulation: Regulatory frameworks for tokenised deposits, stablecoins and digital
settlement assets continue to evolve across jurisdictions. While progress has been
made, important questions remain around legal treatment, settlement finality,
prudential requirements and the use of tokenised money in cross-border
transactions.
Liquidity: Tokenised money does not create liquidity; rather, it may improve how

liquidity is mobilised and deployed. Potential reductions in pre-funding
requirements and trapped liquidity depend on sufficient market participation,
transaction volume and interoperability across networks. Today, many initiatives
remain fragmented across separate platforms and participant groups, limiting the
extent to which liquidity benefits can be realised. Moreover, unlike CLS, most
tokenised settlement networks do not yet provide multilateral netting at scale ,

meaning some participants could face higher gross funding requirements even as
settlement processes become more automated. This is important because
multilateral netting and the resulting liquidity savings have been identified as
critical features of wholesale FX payment and settlement systems, including in
discussions at the Jackson Hole Economic Policy Symposium.
Interoperability and integration: Many of the potential benefits depend on the
ability of different forms of money, payment systems and settlement

infrastructures to operate together. Successful adoption will require integration
with existing banking, payment, custody, accounting and regulatory reporting
processes. Programmable and traditional infrastructures are likely to coexist,
creating additional operational and implementation challenges.

Market infrastructure: The benefits of programmable settlement are greatest when

Operational resilience and cybersecurity: Tokenised settlement infrastructures

Governance arrangements, including liability allocation, system upgrades and
contingency procedures, remain critical to ensuring trust and resilience.

number of tokenised money issuers, settlement platforms or infrastructure
providers, potentially creating new forms of concentration risk. At the same time,
the proliferation of non-interoperable networks may fragment liquidity across
multiple platforms and tokenised money instruments, reducing the fungibility and
efficiency that underpin effective settlement.
Data, privacy and compliance: The transparency of shared ledgers can improve

auditability and oversight but may also create challenges around confidentiality,
commercial sensitivity and data protection. Compliance obligations, including
AML/CFT controls, sanctions screening and cross-border regulatory requirements,
remain significant considerations and may be difficult to implement consistently
across multiple networks and jurisdictions.
Financial stability and monetary considerations: As tokenised money adoption
grows, regulators have raised broader concerns around financial stability, including

the implications of large-scale migration into digital settlement assets, the
concentration of reserve assets, and changing funding dynamics within the
financial system. In some jurisdictions, particularly emerging economies, the
growing use of foreign-currency-denominated stablecoins has also raised
concerns around monetary sovereignty and the effectiveness of domestic
monetary policy transmission.

Existing infrastructure plays an important role in mitigating settlement risk in

wholesale FX markets. CLS combines Payment-versus-Payment (PvP) settlement
with multilateral netting and liquidity optimisation, generating liquidity savings of
approximately 99% for participants. However, coverage is not universal, and a
significant share of global FX activity continues to settle outside PvP arrangements
due to constraints related to currency coverage, participant access, operating
windows and market structure.

scale. However, this will depend not only on technological capability but also on the

arrangements and interoperability with existing payment and settlement systems.

risk reduction and risk transformation.

Appendix A: Detailed classification of tokenised commercial
bank money
Several public-sector and industry initiatives have proposed different frameworks
for classifying tokenised commercial bank money. The Bank of England describes
tokenised deposits as “deposit claims represented on programmable platforms”
that enable new techniques for payments and settlement. It distinguishes between

non-transferable and transferable tokenised deposits. Non-transferable tokenised
deposits resemble existing deposits and may still require settlement in central bank
money when transferred between customers of different banks. Transferable
tokenised deposits, by contrast, could be self-settling upon transfer, with the
recipient becoming a customer of the issuing bank. This distinction matters for FX
because it affects whether tokenised deposits can circulate across banks directly

or whether interbank settlement in central bank money is still required to preserve
singleness and finality.
Project Guardian uses the broader term tokenised bank liabilities to refer to
tokenised representations of commercial bank money on a shared ledger, without
asserting any specific legal or regulatory interpretation. Tokenised deposits are
therefore a specific form of tokenised bank liability. It further classifies tokenised
bank liabilities along two dimensions: token level and ledger level. At the token

level, it distinguishes between digital twin tokens and digital native tokens. Digital
twin tokens represent ownership interests in underlying traditional bank liabilities,
with ownership rights represented on-chain through digital tokens. Digital native
tokens are issued and custodied natively on a shared ledger, which serves as the
primary record of ownership and rights. The distinction therefore relates to the legal
and operational representation of the underlying bank liability rather than to

transferability, as both forms can potentially support programmable payments and
settlement.
At the ledger level, Project Guardian distinguishes between three deployment
models. A single-bank ledger is a shared ledger operated within the same banking
group, enabling transfers between customers, entities or branches connected to
that bank's infrastructure. Between-bank models enable tokenised bank liabilities
issued on separate banks' shared ledgers to be used for payments and settlement

between customers of different banks.  Interlinking network models  connect
distinct networks, each potentially having its own governance framework and
design, allowing tokenised assets to move across a broader ecosystem of
participating institutions and currencies.
Beyond the terminology used by Project Guardian, industry participants have

らに上振れ、長期金利も3％台定着となろう 。なお、金融環境
について細部をみれば、 銀行の預金獲得競争の激化など変
化は着実に出てきており 、貸出統計に加えてヒアリング情報
なども含め、総合的に見ていくことを考えていくべきだ。
一方、下振れリスクは、政府の市場安定化策の強化 だ。流動
性供給入札での減額や、 9月PD懇での10年債発行減額の観
測が債券市場では意識されている 。実現すれば、タームプレ
ミアムを抑制し、長期金利の上昇余地は小さくなろう 。今後の

観測報道を注意してみていくべきだ 。ただし、こうした対応は
止血策に近く、 長期金利のすう勢はやはり景気 ・物価や日銀、
米国金利を巡る債券市場の思惑によって左右されるだろう。
2. 40年金利の動向と見通し
一方、40年金利の上昇については、10年との金利差がジワリ
と拡大しており、超長期債市場の需給はやや軟調とみられる 。
高市政権の食料品の消費減税発表により 、財政リスクが意
識されていることが一因だ 。実際、40年物ASW はジワリと上
昇しており、背景には財政へのリスク警戒もあるとみられる。
しかし、40年物ASWの水準自体は5月と比べれば相応に低い
（図表2）。CDSも横ばい圏で推移し 、少なくとも信用リスク指
標からは 、財政リスクへの意識が顕著に高まっているとまで
は言い難い。先週行われた20年債入札も無難通過となった。
そのため 、超長期金利の上昇は 、米国など世界的な超長期
金利上昇に連れられた 面もあろう。中東情勢の再悪化による
インフレや財政悪化懸念を受け 、米国の30年物利回りが5％

台を付けた。NY連銀のACMモデルで10年物タームプレミアム
は7月以降上昇傾向に転じており 、リスクプレミアムが市場で
意識されている。これは、日本の超長期ゾーンの先高観の高
まりに寄与したと考えられる。
今後を見るうえで注意すべきは 、財政懸念の高まりが国債需
給悪化に直結していない面もあるという点だ 。前述したように 、
日本のドル建て 40年物ASW（通貨ベーシスで調整） は5月以
降に低下し、米国の30年物ASWも足下低下している （図表3）。
米国政府が国債買い戻し策を発表するなど 、長期金利上昇
への警戒姿勢を鮮明にし 、足下の米国30 年物ASW上昇には
歯止めがかかっている 。現在のASW は歴史的にみて高水準
のため、米国財政懸念が根強いことに変わりはないが、 中東
情勢が再悪化している中でも、財政悪化懸念による国債のパ
ニック売りのような事態が起こっているわけではない。
そして、それは日本も同様だ 。高市政権の発足以降、 積極財
政に対する懸念が沸騰する場面もあったが、 5月以降は日本

の40年物ASW（ドル建て）の水準は、 国債のネット供給減（ 政
府の発行減）などを受けて低下し 、単純比較では足下で米国

も高水準が続いていたが 、海外投資家からみた日本の超長
期債の相対的な割安感は後退していると言える。
この点は、先行きの超長期金利を見るうえで 、海外投資家に
よる買い支えが以前ほど期待しづらくなってきていることを示
唆する。今後は国内投資家の需要が鍵を握る面が大きい。
2026年入り後は、生保の投資需要の持ち直しや株高によるリ
バランス需要から信託の買いも増えている 。財政懸念や日銀
の買入れ縮小は続くも 、景気減速に伴うターミナルレート織り
込みの後退に加え、超長期債の発行減額や政府の市場安定
化策への期待（GPIFによる投資増への期待など ）が、超長期
債需要を下支えするとみている。

確定拠出年金に「ロボアド」は導入できるのか 
～制度設計に向けた論点～ 
 政策調査部 主席研究員  奥田 宏二

（要旨） 
○ 政府は 2026 年 7 月の「成長投資を促進するための金融戦略―資産運用立国のアップグレー
ド―」で、確定拠出年金（DC）におけるロボアドバイザーサービス（投資一任契約）の取扱
いを整理する方針を示した。元本確保型商品のみで運用する加入者が約2 割を占めることへ
の問題意識から、加入者のリスク許容度に応じた適切な商品選択やリバランスをサポートす
る狙いがある。 
○ 現行法は、個人が「自己の責任において運用の指図を行う」ことを制度の基本的な枠組みと
して掲げている。運用商品の選択だけでなく、それぞれへの配分額も加入者自身が決める仕
組みであり、投資一任の導入は、誰が運用判断を担うのかという確定拠出年金法（DC 法）の
根幹に関わり得る。過去にも業界団体から導入を求める要望があったが、厚生労働省は、DC
は他者に運用を任せる制度ではないとして「対応不可」としてきた。

DC法第23 条第1 項は、預貯金や有価証券などの運用方法を列挙する一方、第6 号に、それ以外
でも投資者保護など政令で定める要件を満たす契約を対象にできる規定を置いている。現行の施

方法を追加する余地が残されていると考えられる。 
2018 年に厚労省が投資一任を認めなかった際には、第1 条と第23 条の二つを根拠に挙げてい
た。こうした法令の構造を踏まえると、今回の成長投資金融戦略の記述は、第23 条の「運用の方
法」から制度化する可能性を考える手掛かりになる。 
仮に投資一任契約をDCの「運用の方法」の一つとして位置付ければ、加入者が投資一任サービ
スを選び、そこに充てる額を決めるという仕組みが考えられる。加入者自身が運用方法を選択
し、配分額を決定する点では、第25 条が定める運用指図の形式を維持できる余地があるのではな
いか。加入者が運用の指図を行ったものとみなすことで制度の枠組みを維持した指定運用方法と
も、発想として通じる部分がある。 
ただし、その後の商品選択や資産配分、リバランスを一任先が継続的に判断する点は変わらな
い。仮に第23 条側で手当てする場合、それは何を運用方法として選べるかという問題への対応で
あって、誰が運用判断を行うのかという問題は別に残る。 
最終的な論点は、やはり第1 条との整合性だろう。前述した通り、同条は「個人が自己の責任
において運用の指図を行う」ことを掲げている。投資一任サービスを選択すること自体を本人に
よる「運用の指図」と整理し、その枠内で制度を組み立てるのか。それとも第1 条を含め、加入
者本人による運用判断を前提としてきた制度の基本的な仕組みにまで手を入れるのか。今後の制
度設計では、この点が大きな分岐点になろう。 
政府の「日本成長戦略」は、成長投資金融戦略のうち具体化が必要な施策について、特段の記
載があるものを除き、2026 年度末までを目途に結論を得たうえで、その後速やかに実施するとし
ている。拠出限度額や制度の効率化・シンプル化については「次期年金制度改革まで」という別

定期間（第 1 項）を経過しても運用の指図がないときに通知を行い、通知後の猶予期間（第2 項）
を経過してもなお指図がない場合、指定運用方法を選択し、未指図個人別管理資産の全額を充てる
運用の指図を行ったものとみなすと定める。制度導入時、厚労省は指定運用方法を、一定期間運用
の指図を行わない「例外的な場合」への対応と説明し、改正後も本人が自ら選択した商品で運用す
ることが制度の原則との考えを示した。現在の法令解釈通知も、指定運用方法は加入者の運用指図
権を保護するための規定と位置付けている。
4) Fintech協会は2018 年3 月、規制改革ホットラインに「確定拠出年金制度普及のための施策＜
投資一任サービスの導入＞」を提出した。厚労省は、DC法第1 条と第23 条を挙げ、DCは個人が
自己の責任で運用の指図を行う制度であり、投資一任契約の導入は困難として「対応不可」と
回答した。都銀懇話会も2019 年 10 月に「確定拠出年金における投資一任プランの導入」を要
望したが、厚労省は2020 年6 月、同様に「対応不可」と回答した。

5) 米労働省のQDIA規則では、加入者の年齢や退職予定時期などを踏まえ、401(k)プラン内の投資先
への資産配分を専門家が行う「investment management service」がQDIAの一類型として認められ
ており、managed accountがその例として挙げられている。
6) 加入者が運用を指図する機会を与えられながら指図せず、一定の要件の下で資産がQDIAに投資さ
れた場合、ERISA第 404 条(c)(1)の適用上、加入者が当該資産について支配を行使したものとして
扱う。ERISA第 404 条(c)(5)、米労働省規則 29 CFR §2550.404c-5。
7) DC法第 23 条第 1 項第 6 号は、第 1 号から第 5 号まで以外についても、 「投資者の保護が図られて
いることその他の政令で定める要件に適合する契約の締結」を運用方法とし得る規定を置いてい
る。対象となる運用方法を具体化するDC法施行令第 15 条第 1 項は、運用の方法を表で定めてい

る。表は法第 23 条第 1 項第 1 号から第 5 号までに対応する五つの項（預貯金の預入、信託、有価
証券の売買、生命保険の保険料又は生命共済の共済掛金の払込み、損害保険の保険料の払込み）で
構成され、第 6 号に対応する項は置かれていない。
8) 「日本成長戦略」 （2026 年 7 月 21 日閣議決定）は、 「成長投資を促進するための金融戦略」のうち
具体化が必要な施策について、特段の記載があるものを除き「2026 年度末までを目途に結論を得た
上で、その後速やかに実施する」としている。成長投資金融戦略では、DCの拠出限度額や制度の
効率化・シンプル化については「次期年金制度改革まで」という別の時間軸を示している。
