Eurozone: Inflation rises further, driven by energy prices but inflationary pressures are
easing further; and confidence surveys continue to improve
03/09/2026 3
We have selected two inflation measures (with and without energy) and six survey indicators: 1/ business confidence, as
measured by the PMIs in the manufacturing and 2/ services sectors; 3/ the “input prices” and 4/ “output prices" components

of the composite PMI (in order to identify direct inflationary pressures); 5/ the “suppliers’ delivery times” component of the
manufacturing PMI (a direct indicator of possible supply difficulties and supply-demand imbalance, and therefore, indirectly,
of inflationary pressures in the making); 6/ household confidence, as reflected in its “assessment of financial situation in the
next 12 months” component (in order to capture the impact of inflation on purchasing power). The trends in each of these

indicators are observed relative to month m=0, corresponding to the start of the conflict. Each line does not represent the
level of the indicator, but its cumulative variation compared to month m=0.
The overall picture from the data available for August is positive in terms of confidence surveys and reinforces the
encouraging signs seen in previous months. According to PMI surveys, inflationary pressures continue to ease, while supply-
side tensions have stabilised. Business sentiment in the services sector remains stable, anchoring its previous gains, while

confidence in the manufacturing sector shows a further—and marked—improvement. Another notable and encouraging
development is the recovery in consumer confidence for the fourth consecutive month. The feared relapse in these various
indicators, due to renewed tensions in the Middle East, did not materialize—at least not in August. As for inflation, it
continues to rise (+0.4 percentage points, to 3.3% year-over-year), as expected, driven by energy prices, but the increase

remains limited in that it has not spread to non-energy inflation, which remains stable at 2.2%.
Source: S&P Global, Eurostat, European Commission, Macrobond, BNP Paribas

WESTPAC ECONOMICS
THE WRAP ON ASIA
Mapping out Asia ...
Illiana Jain
Economist, Westpac Group
P: +61 403  908 032
E: illiana.jain@westpac.com.au
• Asian growth forecasts have generally been revised
higher over the past year, supported by resilient domestic
demand and export growth, with the tech sector a dominant

contributor to production and investment.
• Exchange rates have largely failed to reflect these economic
fundamentals. The INR has depreciated 7.1% against the
USD over the past year, while the JPY has fallen 4.1%. KRW
is up 3.7%, but these gains are nascent.
• Regional FX performance is also weak in real effective terms
despite the strength in trade and the potential for regional
economic development.
• Increasingly, capital allocation decisions are offsetting the

support provided by economic fundamentals.
• India’s response has been to actively attract foreign currency
savings back into the domestic financial system. In June, the
RBI introduced a concessional FX swap facility and removed
interest rate ceilings on FCNR(B) deposits.
• By lowering hedging costs, banks were able to offer deposit
rates approaching 7%, creating a powerful incentive for
funds held overseas to be repatriated to India.
• The scheme was successful, with deposits reaching

US$127.2bn and the programme closing a month earlier
than planned due to strong demand.
• The inflows have helped support the INR while reducing
pressure on the RBI to intervene.
• However, the FCNR(B) deposits mature within three to five
years, creating a risk of future capital outflows.
• India faced a similar challenge when around US$34bn of
funds raised during the 2013 taper tantrum matured in 2016.
• Sizeable outflows occurred, but they did not trigger a

disorderly adjustment in INR as the RBI accumulated
reserves and prepared for the maturities in advance.
• The more important development is the changing nature
of India’s external financing. Remittance flows are
increasingly sourced from higher-income migrants in
advanced economies, and these funds are being deployed
as investment capital rather than household savings and
spending.
• As financial markets deepen, future maturities are
increasingly likely to be invested into other domestic assets

rather than repatriated offshore.

WESTPAC ECONOMICS
THE WRAP ON ASIA
• South Korea’s experience highlights how strong economic
performance no longer guarantees currency appreciation.
• Export growth remains robust, profitability among major
technology firms is near record levels, and AI-related
investment continues to support activity.

• Despite this, the KRW has remained weak as Korean
households and institutions increasingly allocate capital
offshore rather than holding returns domestically.
• Financial account dynamics are therefore becoming an
increasingly important determinant of exchange rate
outcomes.
• South Korea recorded a US$46.7bn financial account
surplus over the past year, including a US$29.9bn surplus in
portfolio investment.
• In short, rather than supporting domestic financial markets,

a growing share of Korea’s excess savings is being invested
in international assets.
• Of particular note, Korean holdings of US securities have
climbed to almost US$1tn.
• The scale and pace of these flows help explain why the
KRW has struggled to appreciate on enduring strength
in the trade position. Korea’s challenge is not generating
savings, but retaining them.
• Japan’s headline current account surplus of JPY8.7trn in
Q2 2026 is just JPY5.4trn on a cashflow basis, reflecting

the growing share of overseas earnings being accumulated
offshore.
• At the same time, wide interest-rate differentials have
continued to encourage households and institutional
investors to allocate savings offshore, reinforcing yen
weakness.
• Rising JGB yields and higher FX hedging costs are beginning
to reverse these incentives, making domestic assets more
attractive relative to the foreign alternatives.
• Still, the gap is wide and inertia is strong. A material

appreciation in the yen against the US dollar will take time
and the right circumstances.

Tokenisation has the potential to transform the way financial markets operate, from the 
movement of money and assets to the management of liquidity and risk. In this paper, we 
focus on tokenised forms of money and their impact on FX markets. 
Many emerging use cases for tokenised money are driven by a desire to reduce frictions 
associated with cross-border payments and FX settlement. Stablecoins enable 24/7 
cross-border transfers but remain overwhelmingly US dollar-denominated. As a result, 
they are currently more relevant as digital dollar infrastructure and for bridge-currency 
arrangements, often creating a "stablecoin sandwich" in which the US dollar serves as an 
intermediary between two currency legs, rather than providing a complete solution to the 
two-currency FX settlement problem 
Tokenisation may play a bigger role in transforming the combined securities and FX 
settlement cycles involved in cross-border capital flows. This is an area of increasing 
complexity, as delays or failures in one part of the transaction chain can affect the timely 
completion of the wider transaction, increasing operational, liquidity, and settlement 
risks. For example, following the transition to T+1 securities settlement in North America 
and with Europe preparing to move to T+1, the need to coordinate FX, cash, and securities 
settlement across institutions, jurisdictions, and infrastructures is already increasing. 
Tokenisation enables programmability, which allows atomic settlement and a range of 
operational and control functions across financial market infrastructures, including real-
time liquidity management, margin management, collateral mobilisation and substitution, 
and automated compliance checks. Current implementations have focused primarily on 
supporting corporate treasury and liquidity management activities involving FX. However, 
the settlement of tokenised securities and the associated FX transactions may offer even 
greater efficiencies by enabling synchronised settlement and reducing some of the 
operational, funding, and coordination frictions associated with shorter settlement cycles. 
Interoperability between different currencies, tokenised money arrangements, and 
payment systems remains one of the key challenges facing the development of tokenised 
cross-border payments and FX settlement. Several initiatives are exploring solutions 
involving tokenised central bank reserves or hybrid models that combine tokenised 
commercial bank money with central bank money. These may be particularly relevant for 
multi-currency settlement frameworks, preserving the two-tier monetary system while 
supporting settlement finality and more integrated cross-border processes. In parallel, 
other initiatives are building infrastructure that enables different forms of money, payment 
systems, and settlement architectures to operate together. 
Tokenisation promises substantial benefits but unintended consequences are worth 
considering. Beyond faster settlement, wholesale FX markets also depend on liquidity 
efficiency. This means banks and other financial institutions can settle large transaction 
volumes safely while using less liquidity. Existing infrastructures such as Continuous 
Linked Settlement (CLS) combine settlement risk mitigation with significant liquidity 
savings through multilateral netting and liquidity optimisation mechanisms. Unlike CLS, 
most current tokenised settlement models do not yet provide multilateral netting at

Introduction
Tokenised money refers to digital representations of money that are recorded and
transferable on shared or distributed ledger infrastructure. This includes both state
and privately issued fiat-denominated forms of tokenised money, including
stablecoins, tokenised commercial bank deposits and tokenised central bank
money. 1 The potential role of tokenised money is increasingly being explored by

both corporates and institutional investors. Several use cases have emerged
ranging from cross-border payments, wholesale settlement, treasury and liquidity
management, trade finance and capital market infrastructure. More recently,
attention has expanded beyond using tokenised money for cross-border payments
towards models that can support the coordination and settlement of FX
transactions.
While tokenised money can support more efficient payments, FX settlement often

requires the conditional coordination of payments across currencies. Unlike a
simple payment, FX settlement requires the exchange of two currencies to occur
simultaneously, creating a need for a synchronisation or programmability layer
that enables Payment-versus-Payment (PvP) settlement and reduces settlement
risk. Tokenised money operating on programmable ledger-based infrastructure
can support this by embedding rules and conditions directly into settlement

processes, facilitating the settlement of both tokenised money and other tokenised
assets.
The settlement process, particularly  for wholesale FX transactions involving
financial institutions and large corporations, is exposed to settlement risk, often
referred to as Herstatt risk. This arises when one party delivers its currency but the
counterparty fails to fulfil its obligation. Cross-border transactions are particularly
vulnerable because time zone differences can create a window in which one side

has settled while the other has not. Over recent decades, this risk has been
materially reduced in major currencies through infrastructure such as CLS, helping
to support a large, liquid and operationally resilient FX market.
CLS mitigates FX settlement risk through PvP settlement, ensuring that one
currency is transferred only if the corresponding currency is delivered, while
reducing funding requirements via multilateral netting and liquidity optimisation

tools, generating liquidity savings of over 96% for settlement members. This
liquidity efficiency is important in wholesale markets because it determines the
amount of liquidity required for the system to settle its full volume of transactions
safely.
However, these benefits are not universal. Many emerging-market currencies
subject to current- or capital-account restrictions remain outside the CLS
ecosystem and continue to rely on correspondent banking networks or local

clearing systems. Settlement in these markets often involves bilateral pre-funding,
fragmented payment chains and complex liquidity management, creating
operational and funding frictions that can increase the cost and complexity of
cross-border transactions.

The role of tokenised money in FX is closely linked to the broader tokenisation of

interoperability and integration with existing financial market infrastructures to
deliver meaningful improvements in cross-border settlement and capital market

significantly higher. However, the scope of these initiatives is beginning to broaden.

As tokenised money infrastructure matures, some projects are moving beyond
payment efficiency towards models that could support FX settlement.

before assessing the role of tokenised money in FX. It evaluates the opportunities
and limitations of stablecoins, tokenised deposits and tokenised central bank
reserves, and considers whether these innovations can address challenges that

continue to characterise cross-border FX markets.
FX settlement today
FX trading takes place primarily in the over-the-counter (OTC) market, where banks,
dealers, electronic platforms and other market participants facilitate the exchange
of currencies. Once a trade is executed, it enters the post-trade process, during
which trade details are confirmed bilaterally or cleared through a central
counterparty (CCP), payment instructions are exchanged, and settlement

obligations are established (Figure 1). This process spans the period between trade
execution and final settlement, during which exposures are managed, liquidity is

currency it purchased. Depending on the currency pair, FX spot transactions

How CLS works?
Today, CLS achieves simultaneous settlement through a defined funding and
settlement process rather than instantaneous settlement.  Under this process,
settlement is deferred to a daily settlement cycle, enabling multilateral netting

and liquidity optimisation before final settlement occurs. This is important in
wholesale markets because it determines the amount of liquidity required for the
system to settle its full volume of transactions safely. Settlement finality is
achieved through the use of central bank money.

The PvP mechanism also illustrates that a form of atomic settlement already exists
in today's FX market. Once settlement occurs in CLS, it is final and irrevocable,
meaning the transfer of both currency legs cannot be unwound. However, as CLS
notes, atomic settlement can encompass both simultaneity and

instantaneousness, which are distinct concepts. Settlement can occur on an all-or-
nothing basis, with both currency legs settling simultaneously and with finality,
without necessarily taking place at the moment a trade is executed.
The CLS function differs from that of a central clearing counterparty (CCP). While
CLS's PvP mechanism eliminates FX settlement risk,  it does not eliminate
replacement cost ris k, which arises when a counterparty defaults before

settlement and the transaction must be replaced at prevailing market prices. These
risks can be particularly significant for FX forwards and swaps, where the period
between trade execution and final settlement may extend for months or even years.
CCPs such as ForexClear help mitigate counterparty credit and replacement cost
risk through central clearing and margining, whereas CLS is designed to address
settlement risk at the point of settlement by ensuring the simultaneous exchange

of both currency legs.
Settlement risks significantly reduced but not eliminated
According to the latest BIS Triennial Survey, banks and other financial institutions
account for approximately 95% of global FX turnover, compared with around 5% for
non-financial firms. Around 65% of financial-sector FX activity is cross-border in
nature. As a result, wholesale FX settlement is overwhelmingly driven by financial
institutions, underscoring the importance of robust cross-border settlement

infrastructure.
Although infrastructure such as CLS has significantly reduced settlement risk, its
coverage is not universal. CLS currently supports 18 currencies, and a significant
share of global FX activity continues to settle outside PvP arrangements. Results
from the BIS Triennial Survey show that just over US$5 trillion, representing 36% of
average daily FX settlement activity, was settled through PvP mechanisms that
eliminate settlement risk. A further US$7.6 trillion (54%) was settled through

arrangements such as pre-settlement netting that reduce, but do not eliminate,
settlement risk. Meanwhile, more than US$1.4 trillion (10%) of daily FX settlement
continued to occur on a gross bilateral basis through traditional correspondent
banking relationships, leaving participants fully exposed to settlement risk
(Figure 3).

FX settlement frictions: corporations versus institutional investors
The challenges associated with FX settlement are often most acute when
transactions are cross-border, requiring the movement of currencies across

different jurisdictions, payment systems, market infrastructures and operating
hours. These challenges can arise whether FX settlement supports an underlying
commercial payment or forms part of a broader securities transaction.
Corporations and FX
For corporations, FX spot settlement is typically a standalone transaction that
supports cross-border trade, commercial payments and treasury activities. Cross-
border payments and liquidity management are closely linked to the efficient

functioning of the FX market. Corporates frequently need to convert currencies to
support international trade, meet cross-border payment obligations and fund
activities across multiple jurisdictions. More broadly, treasury functions rely on the
seamless movement of currencies to manage cash positions, fund subsidiaries and
address short-term liquidity needs.
Effective liquidity management facilitates domestic and cross-border payment
obligations, supports the optimisation of cash flows and enables corporates to

respond to changing funding requirements. These activities often involve access to
wholesale FX markets to convert currencies and mobilise liquidity across
jurisdictions. During periods of market volatility, the ability to execute FX
transactions and move liquidity efficiently becomes particularly important,
allowing institutions to respond swiftly to changing market conditions and
maintain confidence in their operations.

FX settlement frictions: These include settlement risk where transactions cannot

through CLS on behalf of corporate clients, the associated commercial payments
generally continue to rely on correspondent banking networks, SWIFT messaging
and domestic payment systems. As a result, although CLS can mitigate settlement
risk for eligible FX trades, many cross-border payment frictions remain, including

delays, cut-off times and intermediary costs.
US dollar payments, for example, are ultimately settled through infrastructures
such as Fedwire or CHIPS. Since relatively few non-US banks have direct access to
these systems, many rely on correspondent banking relationships to access dollar
liquidity and payment services. Corporates, in turn, have no direct access and must
depend on commercial banks to intermediate these transactions.
These challenges are distinct from wholesale FX settlement and relate primarily to

the underlying payment process. For many corporations, the costs and operational
frictions associated with cross-border payments may exceed those arising from the
FX conversion itself.
Institutional investors and FX
For institutional investors buying or selling securities across jurisdictions, FX spot
settlement is part of a broader cross-border securities transaction, where FX
settlement, cash movements and securities settlement must be coordinated across

multiple intermediaries and jurisdictions. When an investor purchases a foreign
equity or bond, they must first obtain the currency required to complete the
transaction. As a result, cross-border investments involve two interconnected
settlement processes: FX settlement, where one currency is exchanged for another
(PvP), and securities settlement, where cash is exchanged for the asset through a
Delivery-versus-Payment (DvP) mechanism. Where currencies are not eligible for

settlement through PvP infrastructures such as CLS, investors may rely on
correspondent banking networks to settle the FX transaction. In these cases, the
two currency legs are typically settled through separate payment systems, leaving
participants exposed to residual settlement risk.
International investors do not typically interact directly with domestic market
infrastructures such as DTCC in the United States. Instead, they access these
markets through custodians, which act as intermediaries between investors and

local settlement systems. Custodians are responsible for safekeeping assets,
managing settlement instructions, coordinating cash movements and arranging
funding across jurisdictions. When a foreign security is purchased, the custodian
must ensure that the appropriate settlement currency is available before the
transaction can be completed through the relevant central securities depository.
This creates a dependency between the FX funding trade and the securities

settlement process, meaning that delays or frictions in obtaining the required
currency can have direct implications for the timely settlement of the underlying
security. The ongoing transition from T+2 to T+1 security settlement cycles, and the

Key challenges for institutional investors under compressed timelines
The move to shorter securities settlement cycles has increased the operational
challenge of coordinating processes. For example, under T+1, a UK investor

purchasing a US equity at the market close (9pm London time) may have only
around two hours before the CLS submission cut-off at 11pm to determine the final
USD funding requirement, execute the associated GBP/USD transaction and
submit it to CLS for settlement. Once the FX transaction settles and USD funds are
made available to the custodian, the securities transaction can proceed through the
relevant securities clearing and settlement infrastructure. Missing this window

may require investors to pre-fund transactions, creating cash drag and unwanted
currency exposure, or settle the FX transaction outside CLS, potentially increasing
settlement risk and transaction costs.
Cross-border transactions involve a range of operational and settlement
challenges, particularly under compressed timelines. Local currency cut-off times
constrain the window available to execute and settle FX transactions, while
settlement risk remains where trades cannot be processed through CLS and must

settle bilaterally. Currency controls and market restrictions may further complicate
settlement in certain emerging-market currencies and necessitate pre-funding.
Shorter settlement cycles also leave less time for trade matching, confirmation,
settlement instruction processing and the resolution of failures, increasing the
importance of coordinating multiple post-trade processes.2
As FX settlement, cash movements and securities settlement occur across

separate infrastructures and timelines, shorter settlement cycles increase the
importance of post-trade coordination. Although the  Global Foreign Exchange
Committee (GFXC) described North America's transition to T+1 as operationally
smooth from an FX perspective, this reflected extensive industry preparation.
Similar challenges are likely to arise in Europe and the UK, where a more
fragmented market structure may add complexity. As settlement windows

compress, market participants have less time to execute FX transactions, source
funding and complete settlement obligations, making cut-off times and cross-
border coordination increasingly important.
Shorter securities settlement cycles may not always require instantaneous FX
settlement. However, for CLS-eligible currency pairs, a move to T+1 compresses
the time available to execute FX transactions and complete funding processes
across institutions and jurisdictions. While shorter cycles can reduce counterparty

exposure and costs, they also increase the need for automation and efficient post-
trade coordination. A further move to T+0 would amplify these challenges ,
sharpening the trade-off between risk reduction and operational efficiency.
Similar coordination challenges are emerging across securities, derivatives and
collateral markets as settlement cycles shorten. BIS (2020) estimates that securities
trade processing costs USD 17–24 billion annually, underscoring the broader cost

of fragmented post-trade workflows. Technologies that improve automation,
synchronisation and risk reduction in securities processing are also relevant for FX.

When settlement processes are fragmented across multiple institutions,
jurisdictions and infrastructures, delays or failures in one part of the transaction
chain can affect the timely completion of the wider transaction, increasing
operational, liquidity and settlement risks. Against this backdrop, interest has
increasingly turned to whether tokenised forms of money can improve the
coordination and synchronisation of settlement processes across institutions

and market infrastructures.
Tokenised money on programmable infrastructure
Tokenised money on programmable infrastructure offers a potential approach to
addressing some of the challenges outlined in the previous section. Tokenised
money refers to digital representations of money that are recorded and transferable
on shared or distributed ledger infrastructure. In many current implementations, its
use is enabled by two key elements: distributed ledger technology (DLT) and

programmability. DLT provides a shared infrastructure on which multiple
institutions can record and observe ownership changes in a common ledger,
potentially reducing the need for reconciliation across separate systems.
Programmability refers to the ability to embed executable logic, such as
conditions, rules and constraints, directly into instruments, or via smart
contracts. Such functionality can range from basic automation to more
sophisticated smart contract applications.

At the simplest level, programmability can automate settlement processes by
ensuring that transactions are executed only when predefined conditions are met,
such as Delivery-versus-Payment (DvP) in securities transactions and Payment-
versus-Payment (PvP) in FX settlement. More advanced applications can support
operational and control functions across financial market infrastructure, including
real-time liquidity management, margin management, collateral mobilisation and

substitution, and automated compliance checks. At a more sophisticated level,
programmability can coordinate multiple parties and obligations within a single
workflow, enabling applications such as trade finance arrangements, treasury and
cash management, automated disbursements and other multi-party payment
processes. These capabilities extend beyond process automation by embedding
business, risk and compliance logic directly into financial transactions (CCAF and

Fii, 2026). An emerging but largely underdeveloped area is the integration of
tokenised money with artificial intelligence and autonomous systems, where
programmable money interfaces could enable AI agents to initiate, manage and
execute financial transactions within predefined controls.
The approach to settlement risk mitigation in a tokenised environment differs from
the current CLS model, which achieves Payment-versus-Payment (PvP) settlement
through a centrally coordinated process operating within defined funding and

settlement windows. While CLS ensures that one currency is exchanged if and only
if the corresponding currency is delivered through a centralised settlement
mechanism, tokenised infrastructures could, in principle, embed PvP conditions
directly into the settlement process through programmable rules or smart
contracts on a shared ledger, enabling settlement to occur automatically when
predefined conditions are met. However, tokenised settlement models need not

rely solely on transaction-by-transaction atomic settlement. Some emerging
platforms, such as ClearToken, also incorporate queues, netting arrangements and
liquidity-saving mechanisms.

The three routes for tokenised money
In practice, the use of tokenised money for financial institutions and corporations

is emerging through three principal forms of tokenised money. The first is
stablecoins, privately issued digital tokens typically backed by cash, bank deposits
or high-quality liquid assets. The second is tokenised bank liabilities, including
tokenised deposits, which represent commercial bank money in tokenised form
and remain embedded within the existing banking system. The third is tokenised
central bank money, which represent tokenised central bank reserves, also

sometimes referred to as wholesale central bank digital currencies (wCBDCs).
While these models differ in their issuer, regulatory treatment and underlying
infrastructure, they all seek to combine the trust associated with traditional forms

Stablecoin adoption is increasingly moving beyond the lowest-cost blockchain
towards a multi-network ecosystem spanning Ethereum, Solana, Polygon, BNB
Chain and other networks. This reflects growing demand for compliance,

programmability and institutional-grade infrastructure alongside low transaction
costs. However, it also highlights an important limitation: stablecoins do not
automatically solve interoperability. Instead, the challenge increasingly becomes
enabling value to move seamlessly across multiple networks and settlement rails.
Stablecoin liquidity remains fragmented across issuers, networks and
jurisdictions, while regulatory treatment continues to evolve across markets. In

addition, institutional adoption requires custody solutions, compliance
frameworks, treasury integration and industry standards comparable to those
available for traditional bank money. As a result, while stablecoins can reduce some
correspondent banking frictions, they do not by themselves provide a
comprehensive solution to PvP settlement across non-CLS currencies.
Despite these complexities, stablecoins are beginning to attract interest from
banks for institutional and corporate cross-border settlement. A notable example is

Nomura's partnership with Circle, which aims to explore the use of fiat-backed
stablecoins such as USDC in Japan as part of next-generation market
infrastructure. The collaboration is focused not only on cross-border payments, but
also on FX transactions, treasury operations, fund transfers, on-chain collateral
management and tokenised capital markets transactions. More recently, a group
of 21 financial institutions plan to issue dollar stablecoin in 2027.

BNY Mellon expanded its institutional stablecoin services in 2026 to support
custody and minting of Circle's USDC, reflecting growing client demand for
regulated stablecoin infrastructure rather than the issuance of a proprietary
stablecoin. Société Générale's Forge subsidiary, on the other hand, issued the euro-
denominated stablecoin EURCV and used it within tokenised capital market
transactions.
Regulatory developments have also helped accelerate experimentation in this area.

In Japan, amendments to the Payment Services Act established a regulatory

became the first global US dollar stablecoin approved by Japan's Financial Services
Agency for institutional use in Japan. Recent legislative developments in the United
States, including the GENIUS Act, have also provided greater clarity around
payment stablecoins. Together, these developments have encouraged banks,

payment providers and financial institutions to explore stablecoins as settlement
assets within regulated financial markets

In some emerging market and developing economy (EMDE) currency corridors,

While USD stablecoins may facilitate the payment leg of transactions involving

through a PvP mechanism, the underlying FX settlement risk remains.
Consequently, current stablecoin models primarily improve the transfer of a single
currency rather than addressing the settlement risks associated with the exchange
of two currencies.

Tokenised commercial bank money
A tokenised deposit is not a new form of money, but a commercial bank deposit

represented or issued in tokenised form on a shared or tokenised ledger.
Economically, it remains a claim on a commercial bank, just as a conventional bank
deposit does today. The key difference is that the deposit, or rights linked to that
deposit, can be transferred and settled using tokenised infrastructure, smart
contracts and shared ledgers.

important because it determines whether tokenised deposits can circulate directly

Project Guardian adopts the broader term tokenised bank liabilities and argues that

account-based and token-based representations of commercial bank money, as
well as native and non-native ledger structures. Although these approaches differ

Current implementation models and infrastructure
Current models are developing along two main lines. The first is single-bank or
single-bank-group infrastructure, where blockchain-based deposit accounts or
tokenised commercial bank money are used between customers of the same
institution or banking group. J.P. Morgan’s Kinexys Digital Payments is an example
of an account-based blockchain deposit model rather than a deposit-token model.

It operates on a single-bank ledger, enabling participating J.P. Morgan clients to
transfer deposit balances across J.P. Morgan’s blockchain-based payments rail.
The BMW transaction illustrates this model: BMW executed a programmable on-
chain EUR/USD FX transaction using Kinexys Digital Payments , involving
automated balance checks, conditional auto-deposits, near-real-time FX execution
and transfers between BMW’s Blockchain Deposit Accounts in Frankfurt and New

York.
The second, more complex model is the interbank model, where tokenised bank
liabilities issued by different banks, in different currencies and potentially on
different ledgers, need to be exchanged or coordinated. Project Guardian explores
several approaches to these interoperability challenges. One model uses a liquidity
provider to facilitate the exchange of tokenised deposits in different currencies,
with off-chain FX prices fed into the shared ledger through an FX quote oracle.

Another model, demonstrated by BNY and OCBC, uses Hash-Time Locked
Contracts to coordinate transfers across two private permissioned bank ledgers. A
third model, involving HSBC and OSTTRA, focuses on settlement interoperability,
using a shared-ledger PvP orchestration layer to match, confirm, fund and settle FX
cash flows, helping to reduce Herstatt risk and improve visibility across the
settlement.
Bank-led shared-ledger settlement platforms are also emerging as infrastructure

that can support tokenised commercial bank money. Partior is an example of a
consortium-based model: a controlled environment in which a defined group of
institutions jointly governs and participates in shared infrastructure. It operates as
a bank-led shared-ledger settlement platform for real-time cross-border payments

Market adoption and FX settlement relevance
Commercial bank deposit claims, including tokenised deposits, have progressed
beyond early pilot stages and are seeing notable institutional adoption. While data
on outstanding tokenised deposit balances remains limited, reported transaction
volumes are significant. J.P. Morgan’s Kinexys platform , for example, has

processed more than US$4 trillion since inception and averages more than US$7
billion daily in tokenised deposit movements for institutional payments. Other
initiatives include DBS Token Services, Citi Token Services, the USDF Consortium,
TassatPay, and projects from UOB and Standard Chartered.
The scale opportunity is potentially significant. Tokenised bank deposits and similar
bank-token models could support around  US$100–140 trillion  in annual

transaction flows by 2030, potentially rivalling or surpassing stablecoins in
institutional transaction volumes. This reflects the view that corporates and
financial institutions may prefer programmable forms of regulated bank money for
treasury, liquidity and FX settlement workflows where integration with existing
banking infrastructure, compliance processes and balance-sheet treatment is
important.
Overall, tokenised deposits appear more directly relevant to institutional FX than

stablecoins because they preserve the existing bank-money model while adding
programmability, potentially allowing new settlement capabilities to be integrated
more easily into existing banking, liquidity and risk-management frameworks.
Current implementation models appear more developed for transaction banking,
treasury and corporate payment use cases than for the broader wholesale FX
market, where achieving interoperability across banks, currencies and settlement

networks remains a more complex challenge.
Key challenges
Tokenisation does not, at least in the early stages, fully resolve time-zone
mismatches or the need for pre-funding as noted by Project Guardian. Hybrid
models are likely to coexist with traditional RTGS systems, correspondent banking
infrastructure and fiat-to-token conversion processes for some time, meaning
liquidity may still need to be positioned across both traditional and tokenised
systems.

A practical issue in cross-currency tokenised deposit models is how the FX rate
itself is determined. Project Guardian notes that early-stage on-chain trading
volumes may not be sufficient to generate reliable FX prices purely from on-chain

Tokenised deposits also do not automatically address all FX settlement challenges.
This is particularly relevant in wholesale FX markets, where activity is concentrated
among financial institutions. A key strength of existing settlement infrastructure is
multilateral netting, which significantly reduces liquidity requirements. Replicating
similar levels of liquidity efficiency in tokenised interbank networks remains
challenging. Unlike CLS, emerging tokenised deposit arrangements do not yet

support multilateral netting at scale, meaning participants may need to trade some
liquidity efficiency for faster or more flexible settlement unless netting and liquidity-
management mechanisms evolve alongside the technology.
Settlement finality presents a further consideration. Existing wholesale settlement
infrastructure ultimately relies on central bank money, which remains an important
foundation for wholesale FX settlement. As a result, alternative models that

combine tokenised commercial bank money with tokenised central bank money
may prove more relevant for wholesale FX applications.
More broadly, the success of tokenised deposits will depend on interoperability
across banks and ledgers, sufficient liquidity in each tokenised currency, legal
recognition of tokenised claims, clear settlement finality, robust governance
frameworks, regulatory consistency, and integration with existing systems.
There may also be balance-sheet costs for issuing banks. If tokenised deposits are

treated as more liquidity-sensitive than conventional deposits, banks may need to
hold additional high-quality liquid assets against them, reducing their usefulness as
a source of funding for lending or maturity transformation.

Tokenised central bank money
Rather than tokenising private bank money or relying on privately issued settlement
assets, an alternative approach is to make wholesale central bank money available
on DLT-based infrastructure. In this context, wholesale central bank money used in
DLT experiments is often referred to as wholesale central bank digital currency
(wCBDC) or, more precisely, tokenised central bank reserves. The latter term is

often preferred because it emphasises that the underlying asset, central bank
reserves, already exists in digital form, with the innovation lying primarily in the
technology used to represent and transfer it. While wCBDC is frequently used
interchangeably with tokenised central bank reserves, its definition varies across
institutions and may encompass a broader range of innovations in wholesale
central bank money that are not necessarily tokenisation-based (IMF, 2025).

Central bank reserves are already held digitally by eligible financial institutions
through RTGS systems such as T2 in Europe, Fedwire in the United States and
CHAPS in the United Kingdom. From this perspective, tokenised central bank
money initiatives represent not the introduction of a new form of public money, but
the extension of existing central bank liabilities into tokenised and programmable
environments.3
Although existing infrastructures already eliminate principal risk through DvP and

PvP mechanisms, tokenised reserves may support a more integrated settlement
process by bringing cash and assets onto the same ledger. This allows transactions
to be completed simultaneously and irrevocably using DLT and smart contracts,
while enabling programmability.
A number of initiatives have examined the use of multilateral platforms that make
wholesale central bank money available in tokenised form for cross-border
settlement. Examples include Project mBridge, Project Jura and Project Agorá.

Leveraging shared infrastructure and governance frameworks, these projects have
demonstrated real-time and atomic PvP settlement between commercial banks in
different jurisdictions using tokenised reserves. Smart contracts can further
automate and coordinate multicurrency transactions, improving operational
efficiency.

CBDC, as used by the project, which is synonymous with tokenised central bank
reserves. Developed by the Hong Kong Monetary Authority, People's Bank of

later, the project was designed to enable direct cross-border payments and FX
transactions using wholesale CBDCs issued by participating central banks on a
shared distributed ledger platform. Following a successful pilot involving 20 banks

and 164 real-value transactions, mBridge reached minimum viable product (MVP)
status in 2024, indicating that the platform had progressed beyond proof-of-
concept testing and was capable of supporting real-value cross-border settlement.

Hybrid models
Alongside tokenised central bank money initiatives, Project Agorá is exploring
alternative architecture that retain central bank money as the settlement anchor
while preserving a significant role for commercial bank money.
Project Agorá, launched by the BIS in 2024 in collaboration with the central banks

of France (representing the Eurosystem), Japan, South Korea, Mexico, Switzerland,
the United Kingdom and the United States, explores a hybrid cross-border
settlement model that combines tokenised commercial bank deposits with
tokenised central bank reserves on a shared programmable platform. In 2026, the
BIS reported that the prototype had successfully demonstrated atomic multi-
currency settlement across participating jurisdictions using a unified ledger

architecture linking tokenised deposits and tokenised central bank reserves. The
project also highlighted potential improvements in speed, transparency and
operational efficiency while preserving the existing two-tier monetary system.
Key challenges
Tokenised central bank reserve models, particularly multi-CBDC wholesale
arrangements, are among the most challenging forms of digital money to
implement. Unlike stablecoins or tokenised deposits, they require extensive

coordination across central banks, legal jurisdictions and financial market
infrastructures. Cross-border deployment raises complex questions around
governance, access, interoperability, regulatory alignment, and the legal
recognition of settlement finality. As a result, industry attention has increasingly
broadened beyond pure wCBDC architectures towards hybrid models that
combine tokenised central bank money with tokenised commercial bank money.

Other settlement solutions and related infrastructure
Going beyond the three forms of tokenised money, other settlement solutions and
supporting infrastructures are evolving in parallel. One example is Fnality, a
privately operated settlement system backed 1:1 by central bank reserves. It

provides a central-bank-money-backed settlement asset for wholesale
transactions. The Sterling Fnality Payment System went live in 2023 and received
Bank of England settlement-finality designation in 2024, with the platform
targeting use cases including PvP FX settlement and tokenised asset settlement. It
uses the Bank of England’s omnibus account solution to hold participant funds and
issues DLT-based settlement tokens representing claims on this pool of funds.

Rather than tokenising central bank money directly, the Bank of England's
synchronisation model enables funds held in RTGS to be synchronised with
transactions taking place on external ledgers through a synchronisation operator.
Settlement in central bank money occurs if and only if the corresponding
transaction on the external platform is completed. Through initiatives such as
Project Meridian FX and the Synchronisation Lab, the Bank has explored how this

approach could support Payment-versus-Payment (PvP) settlement for cross-
border FX and Delivery-versus-Payment (DvP) settlement for tokenised securities,
while preserving settlement in central bank money. The Bank has also highlighted
that extended RTGS operating hours may be an important enabler of such
synchronised settlement models.

Technology alone is not enough: risk reduction or
transformation?
Tokenised money is often discussed in terms of faster settlement, reduced

operational complexity and atomic execution. While these technologies may help
reduce certain forms of settlement risk, particularly where payment-versus-
payment (PvP) and delivery-versus-payment (DvP) mechanisms can be embedded
into the settlement process, they do not eliminate the need for liquidity provision,
risk management, netting arrangements or regulatory oversight. In many cases,
the effect may be better described as risk transformation rather than risk

elimination. Settlement risk may be reduced, but liquidity, operational, legal and
governance considerations remain.
Regulation: Regulatory frameworks for tokenised deposits, stablecoins and digital
settlement assets continue to evolve across jurisdictions. While progress has been
made, important questions remain around legal treatment, settlement finality,
prudential requirements and the use of tokenised money in cross-border
transactions.
Liquidity: Tokenised money does not create liquidity; rather, it may improve how

liquidity is mobilised and deployed. Potential reductions in pre-funding
requirements and trapped liquidity depend on sufficient market participation,
transaction volume and interoperability across networks. Today, many initiatives
remain fragmented across separate platforms and participant groups, limiting the
extent to which liquidity benefits can be realised. Moreover, unlike CLS, most
tokenised settlement networks do not yet provide multilateral netting at scale ,

meaning some participants could face higher gross funding requirements even as
settlement processes become more automated. This is important because
multilateral netting and the resulting liquidity savings have been identified as
critical features of wholesale FX payment and settlement systems, including in
discussions at the Jackson Hole Economic Policy Symposium.
Interoperability and integration: Many of the potential benefits depend on the
ability of different forms of money, payment systems and settlement

infrastructures to operate together. Successful adoption will require integration
with existing banking, payment, custody, accounting and regulatory reporting
processes. Programmable and traditional infrastructures are likely to coexist,
creating additional operational and implementation challenges.

Market infrastructure: The benefits of programmable settlement are greatest when
cash, securities and collateral can move on the same, or fully interoperable,
infrastructure. While technological capability is advancing rapidly, broader
adoption depends on custodians, central securities depositories, asset managers,
banks and market infrastructures adapting their operating models and processes.

Operational resilience and cybersecurity: Tokenised settlement infrastructures
introduce new operational dependencies, including distributed ledger networks,
smart contracts, wallets, bridges and external data providers. Coding errors,
cyberattacks, failures in oracle services or vulnerabilities in cross-chain
connectivity can disrupt settlement processes or lead to unintended outcomes. As
networks become increasingly interconnected, disruptions may propagate more

rapidly across participants and platforms.
Legal certainty and governance : The legal treatment of tokenised money
continues to evolve. Questions remain around ownership rights, insolvency
treatment, the enforceability of self-executing smart contracts, dispute resolution
mechanisms and the alignment of technical and legal settlement finality.
Governance arrangements, including liability allocation, system upgrades and
contingency procedures, remain critical to ensuring trust and resilience.

Market concentration and fragmentation:  Network effects may favour a small
number of tokenised money issuers, settlement platforms or infrastructure
providers, potentially creating new forms of concentration risk. At the same time,
the proliferation of non-interoperable networks may fragment liquidity across
multiple platforms and tokenised money instruments, reducing the fungibility and
efficiency that underpin effective settlement.
Data, privacy and compliance: The transparency of shared ledgers can improve

auditability and oversight but may also create challenges around confidentiality,
commercial sensitivity and data protection. Compliance obligations, including
AML/CFT controls, sanctions screening and cross-border regulatory requirements,
remain significant considerations and may be difficult to implement consistently
across multiple networks and jurisdictions.
Financial stability and monetary considerations: As tokenised money adoption
grows, regulators have raised broader concerns around financial stability, including

the implications of large-scale migration into digital settlement assets, the
concentration of reserve assets, and changing funding dynamics within the
financial system. In some jurisdictions, particularly emerging economies, the
growing use of foreign-currency-denominated stablecoins has also raised
concerns around monetary sovereignty and the effectiveness of domestic
monetary policy transmission.
Conclusion
Existing infrastructure plays an important role in mitigating settlement risk in

wholesale FX markets. CLS combines Payment-versus-Payment (PvP) settlement
with multilateral netting and liquidity optimisation, generating liquidity savings of
approximately 99% for participants. However, coverage is not universal, and a
significant share of global FX activity continues to settle outside PvP arrangements
due to constraints related to currency coverage, participant access, operating
windows and market structure.

While tokenised money may help address some of these frictions, its potentially
stronger use case for wholesale FX lies in the wider tokenisation of capital markets.
Tokenised money could enable more integrated settlement processes for tokenised
assets. Reflecting this potential, a recent study identified capital market

the potential to change how securities, bonds and other financial instruments are
issued, traded and settled. The vision is an integrated ecosystem in which tokenised
money is used to settle tokenised securities and other assets, enabling atomic
settlement that reduces counterparty risk and shortens settlement times from days
to seconds.
The Bank of England’s  analysis highlights an important financial stability

consideration. While tokenised deposits extend commercial bank money into
programmable environments, central bank money remains the ultimate risk-free
settlement asset and anchor of confidence in the financial system. Future tokenised
markets may therefore require mechanisms such as RTGS synchronisation or
hybrid models combining tokenised commercial bank money and central bank

the proliferation of multiple forms of tokenised money could fragment liquidity,
reduce fungibility and create new market structure and financial stability
challenges.
Over time, programmable settlement arrangements may replicate some functions
currently performed by existing infrastructures, particularly if interoperable
tokenised deposit or tokenised central bank money frameworks achieve sufficient
scale. However, this will depend not only on technological capability but also on the

development of comparable liquidity networks, legal frameworks, governance
arrangements and interoperability with existing payment and settlement systems.
The transition towards programmable settlement is therefore likely to involve both
risk reduction and risk transformation.

Appendix A: Detailed classification of tokenised commercial
bank money
Several public-sector and industry initiatives have proposed different frameworks
for classifying tokenised commercial bank money. The Bank of England describes
tokenised deposits as “deposit claims represented on programmable platforms”
that enable new techniques for payments and settlement. It distinguishes between

non-transferable and transferable tokenised deposits. Non-transferable tokenised
deposits resemble existing deposits and may still require settlement in central bank
money when transferred between customers of different banks. Transferable
tokenised deposits, by contrast, could be self-settling upon transfer, with the
recipient becoming a customer of the issuing bank. This distinction matters for FX
because it affects whether tokenised deposits can circulate across banks directly

or whether interbank settlement in central bank money is still required to preserve
singleness and finality.
Project Guardian uses the broader term tokenised bank liabilities to refer to
tokenised representations of commercial bank money on a shared ledger, without
asserting any specific legal or regulatory interpretation. Tokenised deposits are
therefore a specific form of tokenised bank liability. It further classifies tokenised
bank liabilities along two dimensions: token level and ledger level. At the token

level, it distinguishes between digital twin tokens and digital native tokens. Digital
twin tokens represent ownership interests in underlying traditional bank liabilities,
with ownership rights represented on-chain through digital tokens. Digital native
tokens are issued and custodied natively on a shared ledger, which serves as the
primary record of ownership and rights. The distinction therefore relates to the legal
and operational representation of the underlying bank liability rather than to

transferability, as both forms can potentially support programmable payments and
settlement.
At the ledger level, Project Guardian distinguishes between three deployment
models. A single-bank ledger is a shared ledger operated within the same banking
group, enabling transfers between customers, entities or branches connected to
that bank's infrastructure. Between-bank models enable tokenised bank liabilities
issued on separate banks' shared ledgers to be used for payments and settlement

between customers of different banks.  Interlinking network models  connect
distinct networks, each potentially having its own governance framework and
design, allowing tokenised assets to move across a broader ecosystem of
participating institutions and currencies.
Beyond the terminology used by Project Guardian, industry participants have
developed more detailed taxonomies to describe how commercial bank money can
be represented on distributed ledger infrastructure. A related industry taxonomy

describes these instruments as blockchain-based deposits: distributed-ledger-
based deposit claims issued by a licensed depository institution and recorded on
blockchain. Within this category, they distinguish between account-based models,
such as blockchain deposit accounts, and token-based models, such as deposit
tokens. The taxonomy also distinguishes between native models, where the
blockchain is the primary record of value, and non-native models, where the

blockchain mirrors an off-chain record.

money, but they differ in how the deposit claim is represented and transferred on
ledger. Blockchain deposit accounts represent deposit balances as account

 台湾経済は世界的な AI ブームを追い風に高成長が続いている。先端半導体や AI サーバーの輸出拡

ェーンを有する台湾は、その最大の受益者の一つとして、当面、高成長を維持する可能性が高い。

 一方、台湾経済のリスクとして、米国などへの生産移転に伴う産業空洞化や電力不足が挙げられる。もっ

 真の死角は半導体産業そのものの競争力低下ではなく、半導体関連産業とその他産業との間で拡大す

3. 台湾経済の高成長は本当に持続するか ～リスク要因の検討～  
それでは、台湾経済の高成長にリスクはないのか。台湾の半導体産業が AI ブームの恩恵を受け続けられるか
どうか、また半導体産業とその他の産業との間で生じる格差が成長を阻害する可能性について検討していきたい。

(1) 台湾の半導体産業の成長は続くか① 米国への生産拠点移転で空洞化の懸念  
まず、台湾の半導体企業が海外進出を進めることで、域内で空洞化が進む可能性がある。もし空洞化が進め
ば、AI ブームが続いたとしても、これまでのように輸出を起点とした好循環は期待できなくなる。日本では、1980
年代後半から産業空洞化が進んだが、その主な要因は円高による輸出競争力の低下だった。一方、台湾の半導
体産業で空洞化が進むことがあるとすれば、主因は地政学リスクと米国の圧力であろう。 
2012 年に中国・習近平政権が成立した後、中国は台湾統一に向けた姿勢を強め、軍事的手段も放棄しない
方針を維持していることなどから、台湾有事リスクへの警戒感が高まった。こうした中、リスクを重くみた需要家から
の要望などを背景に、2020 年以降、半導体など電子部品製造業の対外直接投資は増加傾向にある。 
また、米国・トランプ政権による対米投資圧力の強まりも、台湾の半導体産業の空洞化をもたらす可能性がある。
トランプ大統領は米国内の雇用を増やし、経済成長を加速させようとの観点から、他国との交渉にあたり米国へ
の投資を要求している。その際、半導体の輸入関税引き上げを示唆することで、投資拡大を促している。 
半導体生産の国内回帰はトランプ政権特有のものではなく、党派を超えた政策課題となっている。バイデン前
政権も 2022 年に CHIPS・科学法を成立させ、補助金を用いて米国の半導体生産能力を強化し、技術的な優位
性を確保することで経済安全保障の強化を目指してきた。実際に、台湾の半導体大手・TSMC は 2020 年にアリ
ゾナ第 1 工場の計画を発表した後、投資を積み重ね、2026 年 7 月にも 1,000 億ドルの追加投資を発表するな
ど、足元で総投資額は 2,650 億ドルに達した。台湾の対外直接投資を国別でみると、2023 年に米国向けが中国

こうした所得格差の拡大は、内需の伸びを抑制する要因となる。一般に、所得が1 単位増加した際、そのうちど
の程度が消費に回るかを示す指標を限界消費性向という。高所得者層は所得が増えても消費の伸びが限定的
で、限界消費性向は小さい一方、中低所得者層の限界消費性向は大きく、所得の増加が消費拡大に直結しや
すい。今後の経済成長の果実が半導体関連産業を中心に高所得者層に偏るならば、マクロで見た限界消費性
向は小さくなり、経済成長や所得拡大に比べ、個人消費が伸びにくくなる可能性がある。 
さらに、産業間の成長格差が拡大する K 字型成長が続けば、産業構造の観点から、今後の台湾経済の成長
にリスクをもたらしかねない。台湾経済はもともと半導体関連産業への依存度が高いが、 AI ブームによってその
傾向はさらに強まっている。半導体産業の国際競争力は依然として極めて高いものの、特定産業への依存が強
まれば、世界的な半導体市況の変動や技術革新の方向性の変化に対する経済全体の耐性が低下する恐れもあ
る。 
政府としても、半導体関連産業との格差が広がる一般機械や繊維などの伝統産業、中小零細企業にも成長の
果実を波及させようと、頼清徳総統は 2026 年 5 月の就任 2 周年演説で、8 年間で 1,000 億元規模の「中小零
細企業・伝統産業振興計画」を打ち出した8。同計画は 9 月から始まる立法院での審議入りが目指されており、着
実に実現に向けて動いている。もっとも、年間 125 億元という規模は、TSMC の 2026 年設備投資計画が約 2 兆
元であることを踏まえると、なお小さい。そのため、成長格差を是正する効果は限定的とみられる。依然として台
湾経済の高成長を阻む最大のリスクは、半導体関連産業とその他産業との成長格差の拡大だといえよう。

4. メインシナリオは高成長も、死角は格差拡大 
台湾経済を支える半導体産業は依然として高い競争力を維持しており、AI 向け半導体や AI サーバー需要の
拡大を背景に、輸出と設備投資が成長を牽引する構図は当面続くと考えられる。世界的な AI 関連投資の拡大
が続く限り、台湾経済は主要国・地域の中でも高い成長率を維持する公算が大きい。 
もっとも、台湾経済の真の死角は半導体産業そのものの競争力低下ではなく、半導体関連産業とそれ以外の
産業との間で成長格差が拡大し、経済全体のバランスが損なわれることにある。AI ブームの恩恵は、現時点では
半導体や電子部品、AI サーバー関連企業に集中している。一方、その他の産業の成長力は相対的に低く、産
業間の格差は拡大しつつある。こうした状況が続けば、所得格差の拡大を通じて個人消費の伸びが抑制される
可能性があるほか、成長の果実を十分に享受できない層の不満が高まることも考えられる。 
台湾経済の中長期的な安定成長に向けて、個人消費の拡大は重要な課題である。輸出や設備投資は成長率
を押し上げる一方で、世界景気や半導体市況の影響を受けやすく、変動も大きい。これに対し、個人消費は比較
的安定した需要項目であり、景気変動を緩和する役割を果たす。その意味で、個人消費が経済成長に果たす役
割を高めることは、持続可能な成長モデルを構築する上で不可欠である。現状では、台湾の個人消費がGDP に
占める割合は主要先進国と比較して低い水準にとどまっている（図表 12）。今後、半導体主導の高成長を持続可
能なものとするためには、成長の果実をより幅広い産業や家計へ波及させ、所得の底上げを通じて個人消費を
拡大させることが求められる。

台湾経済の先行きを展望すると、メインシナリオは引き続き高成長である。ただし、その持続性を左右するのは
半導体産業の競争力よりも、高成長の恩恵を社会全体に広く行き渡らせることができるかどうかにある。実際、政
府は産業間の成長格差や人々の間の所得格差の拡大を問題視している。これから議会で審議される2027 年度
予算には、「AI の果実を全国民で共有する」との考えの下、国民一人当たり 1 万元の一律現金給付を盛り込む
など、「経済成長は国民全体がともに創り出したものだが、その成長を実感する速度は各個人で必ずしも同じで
はない」として、格差是正に向けた取り組みを本格化させる方針にある。 
こうした政策が格差の抑制や内需の拡大につながるかが、今後の台湾経済の持続的な成長にとって重要なポ
イントとなる。台湾経済の真の死角は半導体産業の競争力低下ではなく、半導体主導の高成長がもたらす格差
の拡大と、それに伴う内需の弱さにあるといえよう。

5 内需仍然偏弱，政策节奏或加快 
整体而言，去年三季度后基数逐步走低，而 8 月各项经济活动指标仍偏弱，且经济结构

生产端，受去年同期基数走低、极端天气扰动减弱、出口增速有所加快等因素影响，8 月工业

响；二是，投资方面，受用于基建投资的资金同比下降明显 、工业产能利用率下降、房地产
持续调整等因素的影响，8 月三大投资板块降幅都有所扩大。 
展望未来，按照测算，7-8 两月 GDP 同比增速约为 4.3%，仍处在目标区间以下，完成全
年 4.5%的增速难度有所加大。倾向于认为，为了完成全年的经济社会发展目标，政策加码的
必要性正在上升，但短期重心仍会以强化抓落实、 “用好用足”既定政策为主，后续增量政策
也应会“及时谋划”、但并非强刺激、节奏 “边走边看”。后续关注中东局势和油价走势、美联
储加息预期变化、中美高层的会晤情况、国内政策落地节奏等等。

○ 7 月末の日米協調円買い介入後、 再び円安傾向を辿ったドル円相場だが、9 月に入ってからは
自律的な円高が進み、介入時の円高水準を下回る 1 ドル＝154 円程度をつけた。自律的な円
高進展の背景には、日銀が9 月の金融政策決定会合で追加利上げを行った後も、四半期に一
度のペースで利上げを継続するという期待が市場で高まったことが挙げられる。日銀の利上
げ加速期待の高まりは、ベッセント米財務長官の発言なども後押ししている。 
○ テクニカル分析の観点からは、短期的には円は買われすぎていることを示唆している。しか
し、7 月の日米協調介入を受けて、2025 年 4 月以来の円安トレンドをサポートしてきたトレ
ンドは崩れたと見られる。日銀が市場の期待通りのテンポで利上げを行うのであれば、暫く
は円高傾向が続く可能性はある。 
○ ただし、円先物市場における非商業部門の円の売り越し幅は再び拡大、 今年GW の円買い介入
時と同規模となるなど、先行き円安期待は根強い。FRB の利上げ期待も高まっており、日銀
の利上げテンポがドル円相場の行方を左右することとなろう。

2. DC法が定める「運用の指図」 
確定拠出年金の「確定拠出」とは、あらかじめ決められた額の掛金を拠出し、その資金の運用
結果によって将来の給付額が変わる仕組みを指す。給付額があらかじめ定められる確定給付年金

される。 
そのうえでDC法が重視しているのが、誰が運用判断を行うかという点だ。DC法第 1 条の目的規

用の指図は、個別の運用ルールの一つとして置かれているのではなく、法律の冒頭でDCという制
度そのものを特徴付ける要素として規定されている。 
この考え方は理念にとどまらない。第2 条は、企業型年金加入者について、事業主から掛金が
拠出され、 「かつ、その個人別管理資産について運用の指図を行う者」と定義している。企業型年
金運用指図者やiDeCoの加入者、運用指図者についても、それぞれ個人別管理資産について運用の

位置付けられている。

5. おわりに 
これまで導入困難とされてきた投資一任をDCに組み込むとするなら、どのようなアプローチが
考えられるのだろうか。成長投資金融戦略を改めて読むと、 「リスク許容度に応じた適切な商品選
択やリバランスをサポートする観点から、預金や投資信託に限定されている運用商品におけるロ
ボアドバイザーサービス（投資一任契約）の取扱いを整理する」とある。注目されるのは、投資
一任を「運用商品」の枠組みの中で記述している点だ。 
DC法第23 条第1 項は、預貯金や有価証券などの運用方法を列挙する一方、第6 号に、それ以外
でも投資者保護など政令で定める要件を満たす契約を対象にできる規定を置いている。現行の施
行令には、第6 号に対応する契約は具体的に定められていないが（注7） 、法律上は、新たな運用
方法を追加する余地が残されていると考えられる。 
2018 年に厚労省が投資一任を認めなかった際には、第1 条と第23 条の二つを根拠に挙げてい
た。こうした法令の構造を踏まえると、今回の成長投資金融戦略の記述は、第23 条の「運用の方
法」から制度化する可能性を考える手掛かりになる。 
仮に投資一任契約をDCの「運用の方法」の一つとして位置付ければ、加入者が投資一任サービ
スを選び、そこに充てる額を決めるという仕組みが考えられる。加入者自身が運用方法を選択
し、配分額を決定する点では、第25 条が定める運用指図の形式を維持できる余地があるのではな
いか。加入者が運用の指図を行ったものとみなすことで制度の枠組みを維持した指定運用方法と
も、発想として通じる部分がある。 
ただし、その後の商品選択や資産配分、リバランスを一任先が継続的に判断する点は変わらな
い。仮に第23 条側で手当てする場合、それは何を運用方法として選べるかという問題への対応で
あって、誰が運用判断を行うのかという問題は別に残る。 
最終的な論点は、やはり第1 条との整合性だろう。前述した通り、同条は「個人が自己の責任
において運用の指図を行う」ことを掲げている。投資一任サービスを選択すること自体を本人に
よる「運用の指図」と整理し、その枠内で制度を組み立てるのか。それとも第1 条を含め、加入
者本人による運用判断を前提としてきた制度の基本的な仕組みにまで手を入れるのか。今後の制
度設計では、この点が大きな分岐点になろう。 
政府の「日本成長戦略」は、成長投資金融戦略のうち具体化が必要な施策について、特段の記
載があるものを除き、2026 年度末までを目途に結論を得たうえで、その後速やかに実施するとし
ている。拠出限度額や制度の効率化・シンプル化については「次期年金制度改革まで」という別
の時間軸が示されている一方、商品選択の支援策にはそうした記載がない。投資一任の取扱いに
ついては、2026 年度末までに具体的な方向が示される可能性がある（注8）。
